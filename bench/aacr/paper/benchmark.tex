\title{Measuring whether a code review finds the real defects:\\
\large a verified defect benchmark on AACR-Bench, applied to eight
reviewers at their best-shot configurations}
\author{Mateusz Jakubczak \\ Quickchat AI \\ \texttt{mateusz@quickchat.ai}}
\date{Draft of 2026-10-04}

\begin{abstract}
Code-review benchmarks usually score a generated review by how closely it
reproduces the comments the original pull request received. We show that on
AACR-Bench v1.0, a 200-PR, ten-language benchmark~\cite{aacrbench}, this
answer key measures something other than defect finding: on 145 PRs checked
in full, only 12.8\% of the reference comments belong to a real defect; at
least 80\% of the references were written by AI reviewers, including at
least 80 ``human'' references that are rewritten review-bot comments; and
only 31\% of the real defects we verified appear among the references at
all. We build a replacement for the 185 runnable PRs: an answer key of 520
defects (90 high, 145 medium, 285 low severity), each verified against the
code by LLM agents that could not see who raised it, with 3{,}093 rejected
candidates kept with their reasons; and a judge that reads the whole review
and grades every defect as found, partial or missed. On an earlier set of
1{,}295 reviews, judges from two model families agree on 98.7\% of 3{,}640
verdicts (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.97$); neither the key nor the judge has yet
been checked by a human. We also define a best-shot protocol that gives
each compared tool the strongest configuration its documentation ships or
recommends, picked by a rule fixed in advance. Applied to eight reviewers
(seven on gpt-6.1-sol; Claude Code on Claude Opus 5.5), the benchmark
spreads them from 37\% to 79\% of the key's defects, where AACR-Bench's own
metric places five of them within 1.8 points of each other.

The authors build one of the reviewers, QuickReview, and design it for
recall: to find as many of a change's real problems as possible, the serious
ones first. Its frozen build finds 97\% of the high-severity defects (87.5 of
90, a partial find counting half) and 89\% of all 520, ahead of every other
reviewer at every severity; the strongest third-party configuration finds
89\% and 67\%. On Martian's offline benchmark it finds 87\% of the golden
comments an audit judged real, and on Qodo's PR-Review-Bench 52\% of the
functional bugs, the highest recall of the tools each benchmark scores under
one judge (45\% of all Qodo's issues, second to 46\%), and ahead of the same
three third-party harnesses on the same model on both (they find 54--78\%
of the real goldens and 44--46\% of the functional bugs). The price is volume, cost and time: it posts 6.9 comments per PR,
92\% of them correct but 42\% minor, which gives it the lowest Martian F1
and the fourth-highest Qodo F1; it costs \$2.06 per PR at list prices
(median \$1.87; \$2.84 and \$2.48 on Martian's and Qodo's larger PRs), 51\%
more than the build of the eight-reviewer comparison and 4 to 29 times the
third-party tools, and takes a median 17.5 minutes per PR, 1.8 times that
build, against 0.7--2.3 minutes for the others. The key,
the exclusions, the judge prompt and every scoring script are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automated code review is now a product category, and several vendors publish
benchmarks of their own, each of which its publisher
wins~\cite{greptilebench,qodobench,deepsourcebench}. Three problems make most
published numbers hard to interpret.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The answer key.} A benchmark needs to know what a good review
  of a given pull request should say. Public benchmarks usually take this
  from the comments that human (or AI) reviewers left on the original
  PR~\cite{aacrbench,codereviewer,contextcrbench,swrbench}. Those comments
  were never meant to be a complete or correct list of the PR's defects, and
  studies of such data find much of it noisy~\cite{liu2025noisy,lu2025deepcrceval}.
  \item \textbf{The matching rule.} A benchmark has to decide whether a
  generated comment ``is'' a reference comment. Rules based on file and line
  position penalise a reviewer that anchors the right finding a few lines
  away, and precision-style scores punish every true comment that is not on
  the list.
  \item \textbf{Who runs the benchmark.} Vendors benchmark their own tools,
  and tune them on the benchmark. Numbers that are not separated into tuned
  and unseen data, that compare against competitors in weak
  configurations~\cite{melis2018,lucic2018}, or that do not state how the
  answer key was built, cannot be checked.
\end{enumerate}

We ran into all three. We build \qcr{}\footnote{\qcr{} is the public name of
the system; its code, configuration and earlier reports call it
\code{quickreviewd}.}, a production reviewer, and wanted to know whether it
finds the problems a careful senior reviewer would raise before merge.
AACR-Bench~\cite{aacrbench} is the most suitable public benchmark we found:
200 real PRs from 50 open-source repositories in ten languages, with a
published, runnable harness~\cite{aacrrepo}. When we scored \qcr{} with it,
the numbers were low for every reviewer and did not track what we saw reading
the reviews: early prompt iterations raised AACR F1 by about 20\% while the
number of real defects found did not move (Section~\ref{sec:disagree}).

This paper reports the benchmark we built in response and its first full
application (Figure~\ref{fig:howitworks}). We replace the reference list with
a verified defect key for all 185 runnable PRs, grade reviews with a
defect-level judge checked across two model families, and compare tools with
a best-shot protocol that gives each third-party tool the strongest
configuration its own documentation ships or recommends. We then apply it to
eight reviewers.

\paragraph{The headline metric.} We lead with recall: the share of the key's
verified defects a review finds, high-severity defects first, then all of
them. That is what \qcr{} is designed for --- to find as many of a PR's real
problems as possible --- and what matters most before a merge. Precision,
comment accuracy and F1 are reported beside it as the trade-off of that
design, and cost and time per PR beside every result.

\paragraph{Conflict of interest.} The authors build \qcr{}, one of
the eight reviewers, and it ranks first. The same AI assistant that designed
\qcr{}'s benchmark-driven changes also orchestrated the construction of the
key and wrote the judge prompt. Interested parties are known to bias
evaluations in their favour even when the methods look sound:
industry-sponsored studies favour their sponsor~\cite{lundh2017}, and
providers' selective disclosure of privately tested variants biases public
leaderboards~\cite{singh2025leaderboard}.
We therefore record the reasoning behind each methodological choice,
including the alternatives we rejected; we measure the biases we could
measure (Sections~\ref{sec:halves},~\ref{sec:sol}); we list in
Section~\ref{sec:threats} every way we know the method could favour our
system; and we release the key, the exclusions, the prompts and the scoring
scripts so that others can check them. \qcr{}'s own design is described in a
companion report~\cite{qcrreport}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} What is new here, and what is not, is set out
against prior work at the end of Section~\ref{sec:related}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An audit of a benchmark's answer key.} We measure what
  AACR-Bench's reference comments are: how few name a real defect, how few
  real defects they name, and how many of its ``human'' references are
  rewritten review-bot comments (Section~\ref{sec:aacr}).
  \item \textbf{A verified defect key} for all 185 runnable AACR-Bench v1.0
  PRs: 520 defects with severity, sites, evidence and matching notes, 3{,}093
  recorded exclusions, and per-defect provenance that lets pool bias be
  measured (Section~\ref{sec:gt}).
  \item \textbf{A defect-level judge}, with cross-family agreement and
  self-consistency reported (Section~\ref{sec:judging}).
  \item \textbf{A best-shot protocol} for configuring competitor tools: the
  documented configurations measured on PRs no tuning touched, a selection
  rule fixed in advance, and the selection's optimism measured on held-out
  PRs (Section~\ref{sec:bestshot}).
  \item \textbf{An eight-reviewer comparison} under both metrics, with cost,
  uncertainty, bias checks, and worked examples of what the judge credits and
  what AACR's metric rewards (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:disagree}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{What code review finds.} Empirical studies of modern code review
agree that finding defects is one outcome among several, and not the most
frequent. At Microsoft, finding defects was the main motivation developers
gave for review, but reviews turned out to be less about defects than
expected and more about knowledge transfer, team awareness and alternative
solutions~\cite{bacchelli2013}; a later Microsoft report put the point in its
title~\cite{czerwonka2015}. In industrial and student
reviews~\cite{mantyla2009} and in open-source projects~\cite{beller2014},
about 75\% of the issues reviews found concerned evolvability
(maintainability) rather than visible functionality. At Google, developers
named education, maintaining norms, gatekeeping and accident prevention as
what they expect from review~\cite{sadowski2018}. Review coverage and
participation are still associated with fewer post-release
defects~\cite{mcintosh2014}. So the comments a PR received are a record of
what its reviewers chose to say, not a list of its defects, and a benchmark
that uses them as the answer key measures agreement with that record.

\paragraph{Review-comment generation, its metrics and its data.} Learning-based
review automation started from mined review data: models trained to produce
the change or comment a human reviewer asked for~\cite{tufano2022}, scored by
textual overlap (BLEU) with that comment~\cite{codereviewer}. The CodeReviewer
authors themselves note that review comments are ``diverse and non-unique''
and that BLEU is not suitable for the task~\cite{codereviewer}. Manual
analyses then found the data itself noisy: issues in the datasets used to
train and test these models~\cite{tufano2024}, about 36\% of sampled
CodeReviewer training comments vague or non-actionable~\cite{liu2025noisy},
and fewer than 10\% of benchmark comments judged high quality for automation,
with text similarity correlating weakly with defect
detection~\cite{lu2025deepcrceval}. Reference-free metrics such as
CRScore~\cite{crscore} score a comment against model-generated
pseudo-references for the claims and code smells of the change. A survey of 99
papers charts the move from these sub-tasks to end-to-end generative,
increasingly PR-level review~\cite{crsurvey}.

\paragraph{Pull-request-level review benchmarks.} AACR-Bench~\cite{aacrbench,aacrrepo}
already recognises that raw PR comments are a noisy, incomplete ground truth:
it augments human review threads (rewritten by an LLM) with comments from six
LLMs, each kept or dropped by at least two annotators, and matches generated
to reference comments by line overlap and an LLM judge. Our finding is that
the augmented list is still mostly not defects (Section~\ref{sec:aacr}).
SWR-Bench~\cite{swrbench} uses 1{,}000 manually verified PRs from 12 Python
projects (500 with issues, 500 clean), whose ground-truth issues an LLM
extracts from the review discussion and commits and annotators verify; about
two-thirds of those issues are documentation, style or structure, and its LLM
judge reports about 90\% agreement with human judgement.
ContextCRBench~\cite{contextcrbench} builds 67{,}910 context-enriched hunk- and
line-level items from mined issue--PR pairs; CodeFuse-CR-Bench~\cite{guo2025codefusecr}
(601 Python instances) and SWE-PRBench~\cite{kumar2026sweprbench} (350 PRs)
use filtered human review comments as the key, the latter explicitly leaving
correct but unmatched findings unpenalised. Martian's offline benchmark~\cite{martianbench,martiansite}
scores the published comments of commercial reviewers on 50 PRs against 173
golden comments with an LLM judge; we audit its key and run on it in
Section~\ref{sec:martian}. Other benchmarks avoid human
comments as the key: c-CRAB~\cite{ccrab} turns 234 validated human comments
into LLM-generated tests that a review must lead a fixing agent to pass,
keeping 234 of 1{,}313 comments after dropping conversational or untestable
ones and those whose tests failed validation;
CR-Bench~\cite{pereira2026crbench} derives its defects from SWE-bench issues;
MCR-Bench~\cite{zheng2026mcrbench} reports that its LLM judge agrees with
humans far better than BLEU does. Vendor benchmarks take two further routes:
Greptile re-introduced 50 historical bugs into fresh PRs and counted a bug as
caught only by a line comment that names it~\cite{greptilebench}; Qodo
injected best-practice violations and 1--3 functional bugs into 100 merged
PRs (580 issues) and matched outputs with an LLM judge that also requires the
right line~\cite{qodobench}. Each vendor's benchmark is run by the vendor, and
each vendor wins its own~\cite{deepsourcebench}.

\paragraph{AI reviewers in deployment.} Industrial studies measure a
different signal: whether developers act on a bot's comments. Reported figures
range from 73.8\% of comments resolved for a PR-Agent-based reviewer in an
industrial deployment~\cite{cihan2025}, about 40\% resolution for Google's
AutoCommenter~\cite{vijayvergiya2024autocommenter} and 75\% precision for
ByteDance's BitsAI-CR~\cite{sun2025bitsaicr}, to 8.1\% and 7.2\% of comments
accepted at Mozilla and Ubisoft~\cite{olewicki2024revmate}. Studies of AI
review on GitHub find effectiveness varies widely across
tools~\cite{sun2026aiactions} and that most review agents' comments are mostly
noise~\cite{chowdhury2026cra}. Whether a comment is resolved reflects workflow
constraints as much as comment quality, and LLM judges agree with developer
labels only moderately~\cite{karakaya2026}. These signals measure usefulness
in a team; ours measures recall of verified defects, which is narrower and
can be computed offline.

\paragraph{Defect benchmarks and their ground truth.} Benchmarks of real bugs
define the ground truth by a mechanism: Defects4J~\cite{defects4j},
BugsInPy~\cite{widyasari2020bugsinpy} and ManyBugs~\cite{legoues2015manybugs}
isolate real fixes and keep a test that exposes each bug, so only
test-expressible bugs enter; BugSwarm~\cite{tomassi2019bugswarm} mines fail--pass CI pairs, of which a
later review found only 3.6\% suitable for fault-localisation and repair
studies~\cite{durieux2019bugswarm}. Every such mechanism has a documented
error rate when checked by hand: a third of ``bug'' reports are not
bugs~\cite{herzig2013}, only half of SZZ's bug-fixing commits fix
bugs~\cite{herbold2022szz}, and 20--71\% of vulnerability labels in real-world
datasets are inaccurate~\cite{croft2023}; on a relabelled, de-duplicated and
time-split vulnerability dataset a model's F1 fell from 68\% to
3\%~\cite{ding2025primevul}. Label errors in
machine-learning test sets likewise change which model looks
best~\cite{northcutt2021}. Habib and Pradel measured the recall of static bug
detectors against Defects4J and found they miss most known
bugs~\cite{habib2018}; our setting is the review analogue, with a key built by
verification rather than by a test oracle. SWE-bench~\cite{swebench} shows
the life cycle of an executable key: a human-screened subset after many tasks
proved underspecified~\cite{swebenchverified}, then audits finding solution
leakage and weak tests~\cite{aleithan2024,wang2026solved,yu2025utboost} and
memorisation of the repositories~\cite{liang2026illusion}.

\paragraph{LLM judges and agreement.} Using a strong model to grade
open-ended output is standard practice~\cite{zheng2023,gu2024survey}, with
documented biases: sensitivity to the order of
candidates~\cite{wang2024fair,zheng2023}, a preference for longer
answers~\cite{zheng2023}, and a preference for a model's own generations that
grows with its ability to recognise them~\cite{panickssery2024}. Pooling
judges from different model families is one mitigation~\cite{verga2024}. In
software engineering, LLMs approach human agreement on some annotation tasks
but not others, and model--model agreement has been proposed as a signal of
whether a task suits an LLM annotator~\cite{ahmed2025}; LLM judges correlate
with humans on code generation and translation better than on
summarisation~\cite{wang2025sejudge}, and reference-free code judges
outperform token-matching metrics~\cite{tong2024codejudge,zhuo2024icescore}.
Large studies conclude that LLM judges must be validated against human
judgements task by task~\cite{bavaresco2025,shankar2024}, and that high raw
agreement can hide large score differences, so chance-corrected agreement
should be reported beside it~\cite{thakur2025}. Our per-defect verdicts
follow the decomposition of long-form evaluation into atomic, separately
checked claims~\cite{min2023factscore}. We report Cohen's
$\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960}, read on the usual scale~\cite{landis1977}, knowing
that it can be distorted by skewed class prevalence~\cite{feinstein1990} and
that alpha-like coefficients are an alternative~\cite{artstein2008,hayes2007}.

\paragraph{Pooled answer keys.} Our key is a pool: the union of what a hunt,
the references and the reviewers raised, each verified. Information retrieval
met the same problem when it proposed pooling for large test
collections~\cite{sparckjones1975}: with TREC's pooled relevance judgements,
relative rankings proved fairly reliable but recall is overestimated, because
many relevant documents are never judged~\cite{zobel1998}; pools can be biased
against systems unlike those that built them~\cite{buckley2007}, which
motivated measures robust to incomplete judgements~\cite{buckley2004}; and
large disagreements between relevance assessors change system rankings very
little~\cite{voorhees2000}. LLM relevance assessors can match human
labellers~\cite{thomas2024}, and even with only fair label agreement can
reproduce similar system rankings~\cite{faggioli2023}, but when the systems under test are
themselves LLMs the evaluation risks circularity~\cite{clarke2025}. Our
independent-source and leave-one-reviewer-out checks follow IR's practice of
re-scoring a system on a pool built without its own contribution, and, like
that literature, we do not claim absolute recall.

\paragraph{Benchmark methodology, baselines and conflicts of interest.}
Checklists for benchmark quality ask for documented construction, validated
automatic evaluation, uncertainty and a replication
script~\cite{reuel2024betterbench,zhu2025abc,gebru2021datasheets}, and warn
that a benchmark measures a narrower construct than its name
suggests~\cite{raji2021}. Comparisons in machine learning have repeatedly been
overturned when baselines received the tuning budget of the proposed
method~\cite{melis2018,lucic2018,dodge2019}, and variance from data sampling
and configuration choice can exceed the differences
reported~\cite{bouthillier2021}; small test sets make many comparisons
underpowered~\cite{card2020power}. Decisions made by looking at a test set can
overstate results on it~\cite{dwork2015}, and so can picking the best of
several configurations on the data used to report it~\cite{cawley2010}; a
drop on fresh data is not by itself proof of overfitting~\cite{recht2019}.
Evaluations by an interested party have a documented bias: industry-sponsored
clinical studies report favourable results more often~\cite{lundh2017}, and
providers' private testing of many variants biases public
leaderboards~\cite{singh2025leaderboard}; in fuzzing, an SE field where tool
authors evaluate their own tools, every one of 32 surveyed evaluations had
problems~\cite{klees2018}, and later papers still often disregarded the
resulting guidelines~\cite{schloegel2024}. Agent evaluations are urged to
report cost beside accuracy~\cite{kapoor2024agents}. Public benchmarks may also
have been seen in training~\cite{sainz2023,riddell2024}, which time-segmented
benchmarks avoid~\cite{jain2025livecodebench,zhang2025swebenchlive}.

\paragraph{What this paper adds.} We do not introduce LLM judging of review
coverage: SWR-Bench, MCR-Bench and Qodo's benchmark already grade reviews
against a list of issues with an LLM judge, and c-CRAB uses an executable key.
Nor is configuring competitors fairly a new idea in machine learning. What we
add is narrower. First, a measurement of what an existing public benchmark's
answer key contains, including the finding that at least 22\% of its
``human'' references on the 185 runnable PRs are rewritten review-bot
comments. Second, a defect key for that benchmark, built by verifying every
candidate against the code, with each of 3{,}093 rejected candidates kept with
its reason and shown to the judge, and with every defect's provenance recorded
so that pool bias can be measured rather than only acknowledged. Third, a
defect-level judge run under two model families, with per-verdict agreement,
chance-corrected agreement and self-consistency reported (but not yet
human agreement). Fourth, the best-shot protocol applied to commercial and
open-source review tools, with the selection's optimism measured on held-out
PRs. Unlike the vendor benchmarks, we publish the key, the exclusions and the
prompts; like them, we benchmark our own system, and Section~\ref{sec:threats}
treats that as the main threat.

\section{AACR-Bench and its reference key}
\label{sec:aacr}

\subsection{The dataset and its metric}

AACR-Bench v1.0 (repository \code{alibaba/aacr-bench}, commit
\code{ab8af8d5}, dataset file sha256 \code{d8683cb2\ldots}) holds 200
pull requests from 50 public repositories~\cite{aacrbench}. Its harness
scores 196 of them (four have no positive references), which carry 1{,}505
reference comments, a median of six per PR. According to the paper and the
dataset's metadata, 74\% of the references were generated by six LLMs and
kept by annotators (GPT-5.2 alone 25\%); the rest are human review threads
that an LLM rewrote~\cite{aacrbench}. Each reference has a \emph{category}
(code defect 47\%, maintainability and readability 42\%, performance 8\%,

\paragraph{The reference-comment metric.} The harness compares each
generated comment with each reference on the same file. A pair is
considered only if the two line ranges overlap or lie within $k=1$ line of
each other (the harness's \emph{line gate}, \code{line\_k = 1}~\cite{aacrrepo};
the paper itself defines a line match as range overlap). A judge model is
then asked whether the two comments ``express the same concern or
suggestion'' (yes/no). Recall is references matched over all references;
precision is generated comments that matched any reference over all
generated comments. The published paper's judge is
\code{Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507} at temperature 0.7, top\_p 0.95 and
top\_k 20~\cite{aacrbench}.

185 PRs, only 162 of the 520 verified defects (31\%) have at least one AACR
reference among their sources, and 40 of the 90 high-severity ones
(44\%).\footnote{Defects whose \code{provenance.dataset\_refs} is non-empty

references are marked human. At least 80 of them (22\%) are rewrites of
comments that an AI review bot posted on the original PR, not of a human's:

them, each human reference on the 46 PRs where a bot had reviewed was paired
with the bot or human inline comment on the same file, within $\pm5$ lines,
with the highest overlap of content words; every pair at an overlap of 0.15
or more was read, and each was the same concern reworded, often with the
same identifiers and fix. The count is a lower bound, since review bodies
and issue comments were not checked.\footnote{Our research note of
2026-09-30, anonymous public GitHub access only. The 80 references are
listed in \code{bench/aacr/bot\_references.json}.} Under AACR's metric such a

that includes those products is tilted towards them on those PRs;
Section~\ref{sec:copilot2025} measures the tilt for Copilot. Our key is
largely immune: 19 of the 520 defects cite one of these references, 17 of
the 19 were also found by the expert hunt, and every one was checked
against the code.

They are not a criticism of the benchmark's construction effort: a PR's
historical review comments were never produced to be an answer key, and
studies of code review find that most review comments are not about
defects~\cite{bacchelli2013,mantyla2009,beller2014}.

\section{Design principles}
\label{sec:principles}

We fixed the following principles before the full run. Each comes with the
alternative we rejected; Sections~\ref{sec:gt}--\ref{sec:bestshot} give the
mechanics.

\paragraph{Measure what a review is for.} The lead metric is \emph{recall
of verified defects}, graded by severity, plus the share of the review's
comments that are true. \emph{Rejected: AACR's metric alone.} It measures
agreement with a list that is largely not defects, and in our tuning log it
rose by about 20\% on prompt changes that did not change the number of
defects found (Section~\ref{sec:disagree}). \emph{Rejected: dropping AACR's
metric.} It is the only number computed the benchmark's way, and a check
that we are not only measuring ourselves with our own yardstick; we report
it in full, including where other tools beat ours.

\paragraph{Run the production reviewer, not a benchmark build.} Both \qcr{}
arms run its production round with a \textsc{benchmark mode} section
appended to the order (no GitHub, no CI, no memory; write the would-be
review to files; Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The two arms differ only in
a configuration preset (\code{default} or \code{low}) that sets the
reasoning effort of every model. \emph{Rejected:} a benchmark-tuned variant
(a switch that posts every true nit, which AACR rewards, was measured and
not adopted), and running every model at maximum effort (the later
\code{max} preset was not used either). Residual differences from
production (\code{bench/aacr/README.md}): memory is off (a foreign
repository must not read or write the production reviewer's memory); the
session loads a reduced extension set and no same-day feedback addendum;
the order is delivered on standard input with at most one
\code{-{}-continue} retry; a multi-line comment is scored at its single
anchor line; and, in the build of the earlier study, the frozen
\code{pass-baseline} seed still carried the deployed repository's pytest
and Django Ninja conventions (later builds moved them to repository-profile
addenda). The rendered round orders of the two arms on R145 are byte for
byte identical apart from commit shas.

\paragraph{Tune on some PRs, report on others.} Every lever added to \qcr{}
during this work was designed from misses on T40. R145 was selected
mechanically as ``every runnable scored PR not in those sets'' and created
at 2026-09-27 23:34 (commit \code{1ad710b}), after the last
\emph{benchmark-driven} change to \qcr{}; no design decision looked at it.
Three later changes to \qcr{} (2026-09-28/29) came from
unrelated work and did not consult benchmark results; a full re-run after
them reproduced the earlier result within 1.5 defects
(Section~\ref{sec:stability}). Results are reported for T40 and R145
separately (Section~\ref{sec:halves}). Which sets each build's development
used is disclosed at the head of Section~\ref{sec:threats}.

\paragraph{Give every tool its best shot, on one model.} Each third-party
tool runs the way the AACR harness runs it (where the harness has a runner)
or in the closest shape to it, with its own prompts. Where its own
documentation ships or recommends a stronger configuration we measured that
too, and each tool enters the final comparison in the best configuration
found, by a rule fixed before the runs (Section~\ref{sec:bestshot}). All
seven OpenAI-based reviewers, \qcr{} included, then run on one model,
gpt-6.1-sol. Claude Code runs on the model it ships for, Claude Opus 5.5,
and is the one tool whose configuration was not varied
(Section~\ref{sec:bestshot}). \emph{Why:} a benchmark run by the author of
one contestant has every incentive to measure the others in a weak
configuration, a known failure of comparisons against under-tuned
baselines~\cite{melis2018,lucic2018}. Our own earlier study did so in three
places without intending to, and correcting those three was worth 12 to 17
points of recall on S40 to the tools concerned (Codex CLI 44\% to 56\%,
pi-review 52\% to 68\%, OpenCode 43\% to 60\%; Table~\ref{tab:bestshot}). \emph{Rejected:} prompt-tuning the
other tools (we would tune competitors with less care than our own system;
we changed only settings the tools themselves expose and document), and an
equal-compute comparison (holding every tool to one budget would compare
designs nobody ships; we report cost next to every recall figure instead,
as cost-aware agent evaluation recommends~\cite{kapoor2024agents}).

\paragraph{Sandbox everything.} All reviews and judgements ran locally in
Docker, never against GitHub's write API. Containers have a read-only root
filesystem, all capabilities dropped, \code{no-new-privileges}, a non-root
user and pid/memory/CPU caps. Each PR's repository is a read-only bare
store holding only the base and the reviewed head commit, so a reviewer
cannot read the PR's future: without this, a reviewer with repository
access could find the fix commit and report the defect it fixes, the
review-time analogue of the solution leakage found in
SWE-bench~\cite{aleithan2024}. The model login is copied per case into the
container's tmpfs with its refresh token replaced by a dummy. Claude Code
uses its own API key and exists only inside its own image.

\paragraph{Make every number traceable and re-scorable.} Every run records
a build fingerprint; every judge verdict is stored under the key version and
judge model that produced it, so a changed key never mixes with old
verdicts; a failed judgement is an error file counted as ``not judged'',
never as a miss; every rejected candidate defect is kept with its reason.

\section{The defect key}
\label{sec:gt}

\subsection{What counts as a defect}

A defect is a concrete problem the PR introduces or newly exposes, that a
competent reviewer should raise before merge, with a statable consequence:
wrong behaviour, crash, hang, data loss or corruption, security hole,
broken build/CI/install, broken contract for callers, a real performance
regression on a realistic path, a resource leak, a concurrency bug, a
compatibility break, or a fix that does not do what the PR says.
\emph{Maintainability} is admitted only with a concrete future cost (for
example a doc comment the change made wrong, or a duplicated rule that has
already drifted). Excluded: pre-existing problems the PR does not touch or
worsen, unreachable scenarios, things already handled, requests for tests or
docs, style and nits. \textbf{Severity} is graded on the consequence, not on
who proposed the defect or how it was phrased: \emph{high} would block a
merge (a user-visible bug on a normal path, crash, data loss, security hole,
broken build/install/CI); \emph{medium} should be fixed before merge (a real
bug on a less common path, a real contract, performance or leak issue);
\emph{low} is real but fine as a follow-up.

Each defect carries an id, title, statement (mechanism and consequence),
one or more \emph{sites} (path, line range, role: cause, symptom or other),
kind, severity, evidence (quoted code and the path by which it is reached),
\emph{notes} (what a review comment must say to count, and near-misses that
must not), and \emph{provenance} (which candidate sources raised it). This
is closer to the bug descriptions of curated defect benchmarks such as
Defects4J~\cite{defects4j} than to a review comment, but unlike those
benchmarks it has no executable oracle: a defect is established by reading
the code, not by a failing test.

\subsection{Why a verified candidate pool, not a blind hunt}

The central design decision is where candidate defects come from.
\emph{Rejected: the AACR references as truth} (Section~\ref{sec:reflimits}).
\emph{Rejected: a blind expert hunt alone.} A single hunt misses defects: on
R145 the hunt raised 241 of the 379 defects that survived verification in the
first version of the key, so a hunt-only key would have been about a third
smaller. The same gap between what one finder sees and what is there is
familiar from static bug detectors, which find only a small fraction of
known bugs~\cite{habib2018}. \emph{Chosen: a pooled, verified, anonymised
union} of candidates from an expert hunt, every AACR reference and every
comment the benchmarked reviewers posted, each re-checked against the code
by a verifier who could not tell which reviewer wrote what, then an
adversarial critic. The price is that recall is measured against the union
of what somebody in the pool found, which favours pool members, as pooled
relevance judgements do in information
retrieval~\cite{sparckjones1975,zobel1998,buckley2007}
(Threat~1, Section~\ref{sec:threats}). We recorded the provenance of every
defect so that this bias can be measured rather than only acknowledged.

The first R145 hunt was launched blind (no references). The operator then
decided the hunt should also check the references, as leads; the blind run
was stopped after about three minutes (32 drafts written), re-launched with
references, and the 32 drafts were re-checked as candidates. Reviewer
outputs were off limits to the hunt throughout.

\subsection{Extension with four more reviewers (v2)}

After Codex CLI, OpenCode, pi-review and Claude Code had run (on gpt-6-sol
and Opus 5.5 respectively), every one of their comments on all 185 PRs
(1{,}527 candidates: OpenCode 557, pi-review 395, Codex 390, Claude Code 185
whole review texts) was anonymised, shuffled by a hash of the comment text,
and checked against the code and the current key by 31 verifier agents. A
Claude Code review is prose, so each whole review is one candidate whose
claims are checked one by one (which is why the outcomes below sum to more
than 1{,}527). Results: 931 matched an existing defect, 691 excluded with
reasons (572 of them on R145), 16 added. Eleven critics checked every
addition and removed one (4 critic corrections in total). Net \textbf{+15
defects}: R145 v2 has \textbf{394 defects} (56 high / 114 medium / 224 low).
The assembly script refused to write if any existing defect was dropped or
changed, and the version number was bumped so verdicts from the two versions
never mix. The same pass over T40 added no defect, so those files were not
changed; their new exclusions are kept only in the analysis folder.

\emph{Why extend rather than rebuild.} Rebuilding the key with all seven
reviewers in the pool from the start would have given every tool an
equal-sized seat, at the cost of redoing a critic-checked verification. We
extended, and measured the resulting seeding bias directly
(Section~\ref{sec:indep}). The small yield (15 new defects from 1{,}527
comments) suggests the pool was close to saturated for these reviewers.
The saturation curve says the same: on R145, start from the 250 defects
that the hunt or an AACR reference raised and add the seven reviewers one at
a time; averaged over all 5{,}040 orders, they add 69.1, 30.1, 17.3, 11.0,
7.4, 5.2 and 3.7 defects, so the seventh contributes under 1\% of the 394.
The average marginal contribution per reviewer is: default 42.6, Claude
Code 21.9, low 21.7, OCR 19.6, pi-review 14.2, Codex CLI 12.1, OpenCode
11.8.\footnote{Computed from \code{provenance} in \code{truth-ext.json} and
the two candidate keys per PR (\code{wp-data/prov.py}), 2026-09-29. Without
the hunt and the references, the reviewers' union grows 237, 303, 336, 356,
368, 377, 382.} The curve flattens for this pool only; it says nothing about
defects that none of these reviewers would raise, and the final comparison's
runs were not in it (Section~\ref{sec:outofpool}).

\section{The defect judge}
\label{sec:judging}

\subsection{What the judge reads and returns}

One judge session per PR, with read-only access to the repository at the PR
head, reads the PR's defect list (statement, sites and ``counts if'' notes);
the claims already checked and rejected for this PR, each with its reason
(capped at 600 characters each, 14{,}000 in total); and the review as the
author would receive it: verdict, body (capped at 12{,}000 characters) and
every comment with path and line. It returns, in strict JSON checked by a
parser, a verdict per defect, \emph{found} (some comment or the body
identifies the defect with its essential point right), \emph{partial}
(points at the problem or its site with a wrong or missing mechanism or
consequence) or \emph{missed}, and a grade per comment: which defect it
finds (or none), and correct-valuable, correct-minor, partly-correct,
incorrect, or unverifiable. Grading each defect separately against a short
statement of its essential point is the same decomposition that
fact-level evaluation of long-form text uses~\cite{min2023factscore}. The
full prompt is in \code{docs/PROMPTS.md} \S2 and reproduced verbatim in
Appendix~\ref{app:judgeprompt}.

\paragraph{Design decisions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Location-agnostic.} A defect counts as found wherever in the
  review it is said: cause site, symptom site, another line, or the body.
  \emph{Rejected:} a line gate like AACR's, which drops the right finding two
  lines away before any judge sees it.
  \item \emph{Substance over form.} Wording, tone, tags and exact line
  numbers do not matter. The ``counts if'' notes, written by the verifier,
  state the essential point, so a vague comment near the right line does not
  count.
  \item \emph{Partial credit of one half.} \emph{Rejected:} binary scoring,
  which either credits a comment whose mechanism would make the author fix
  the wrong thing, or gives nothing to one that pointed at the site. Counting
  partials as zero moves no reviewer by a point and changes no order
  (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \emph{The judge sees the rejected claims.} A comment repeating a
  rejected claim is graded against its recorded reason instead of being
  re-adjudicated. \emph{Rejected:} hiding exclusions, which lets the judge
  re-accept a plausible claim the verifier already refuted with code. Since
  the exclusions were written from the tools' own comments, this makes the
  judge stricter for every tool and cuts both ways.
  \item \emph{Errors are not misses.} A failed judgement is an error file;
  \code{compare.py} counts it as ``not judged'' and prints the row as
  incomplete. Every reported row is 185/185.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metrics.} \textbf{Recall}: found $+\tfrac12\,$partial, summed over
defects, over the number of defects (``problems found'' is the numerator).
\textbf{Recall by severity}: the same per class. \textbf{Weighted recall}:
severity weights high 3, medium 2, low 1. \textbf{Comments correct}: the
share of the review's comments graded correct-valuable or correct-minor;
because this merges a reviewer that posts many true minor notes with one
that posts few valuable ones, every comparison also reports the full grade
breakdown.

\subsection{Judge reliability}
\label{sec:judgestab}

These checks were run on the earlier study's 1{,}295 reviews (seven
reviewers $\times$ 185 PRs, Section~\ref{sec:sol}); the final comparison's
reviews were graded by the primary judge only.

\paragraph{Two judges from different model families.} The primary judge is
\code{gpt-6-sol} at high reasoning. The same prompt, byte for byte, with the
same inputs and parser, was run a second time on \code{claude-opus-5-5}
over all 1{,}295 reviews. \emph{Why:} the primary judge shares a model family
with six of those seven reviewers, including ours, and LLM judges can prefer
their own family's outputs~\cite{panickssery2024,zheng2023}. Using judges
from different families is a proposed mitigation~\cite{verga2024}.
\emph{Rejected:} a single judge with that caveat stated; a Claude-only
judge, which would share a family with the key builder and with Claude Code.

The two judges gave the same verdict on \textbf{3{,}593 of 3{,}640}
reviewer--defect pairs (98.7\%; 7 reviewers $\times$ 520 defects) and on 628
of 630 high-severity pairs. Raw agreement flatters a three-way verdict whose
classes are unbalanced~\cite{feinstein1990}, so we also report Cohen's
$\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960}: $\kappa = 0.974$ on found/partial/missed, and
$\kappa = 0.986$ when partial and found are merged (99.3\% raw), both in the
top band of the usual scale~\cite{landis1977}. Of the 47 disagreements, 45
involve a \emph{partial} on one side; only 2 are found-vs-missed. Problems
found moves by at most $\pm3$ defects per reviewer, and recall is identical
after rounding for all seven.\footnote{Computed from the two judges' verdict
files (\code{judge/v<ver>-gpt-6-sol-high/} and
\code{judge/v<ver>-claude-opus-5-5/}) for the runs in
\code{FULL-BENCHMARK.md}, 2026-09-28.}

Per comment the judges agree less: the same five-way grade on 3{,}972 of
4{,}455 comments (89.2\%), and the same correct/not-correct split on 91--95\%
of each reviewer's comments. The largest movements are partly-correct
graded incorrect (99) or correct-valuable (91) by the second judge, and
correct-minor graded incorrect (82). Comments correct therefore differs by
up to 5 points between judges; the largest gaps are Claude Code (81\% under
GPT, 86\% under Opus) and OCR (73\% vs 77\%).

\paragraph{Self-consistency.} We re-ran the primary judge once on the same
reviews for 30 random PRs $\times$ all 7 reviewers (210 reviews, 637
reviewer--defect pairs, 742 comments). It gave the same defect verdict on
99.7\% of pairs ($\kappa = 0.994$); both disagreements involve a
\emph{partial}. On the same sample the two judges agree on 99.2\% ($\kappa =
0.984$), so the difference between the judges is barely larger than the
primary judge's run-to-run noise. Comment grades are noisier within one
judge too: the same five-way grade on 92.2\% ($\kappa = 0.87$) and the same
correct/not-correct split on 93.5\%, against 88.4\% and 90.6\% between the
judges on this sample, so about two-thirds of the per-comment disagreement
between judges (7.8 of 11.6 points) is at the level of the primary judge's
disagreement with itself.\footnote{Computed from
\code{judge/wpself-gpt-6-sol-high/} (the re-run, seed 20260929) against the
published primary and second-judge verdicts (\code{wp-data/selfcons.py}),
2026-09-29. One round, 596 model calls, \$7.18 at list price.}

\paragraph{What this shows and what it does not.} The checks show that the
\emph{grading} (whether a given review finds a given defect, and whether a
comment is true) is not an artefact of one model family or of one model's
reading of the prompt: a preference of either family for its own reviewers
would appear as disagreement, and there is almost none. The one visible
same-family effect is on comments correct: the Claude judge is 5 points
kinder to Claude Code's comments, while Claude Code's recall does not move
(62\% under both). This mirrors the finding from information retrieval that
assessor disagreement changes absolute scores more than system
rankings~\cite{voorhees2000}.

They do \emph{not} show that the grading agrees with a human. Both judges
read the same prompt and the same ``counts if'' notes, written by the key
builder, and a bias they share would not appear as disagreement. LLM judges
have been found to agree with human annotators unevenly across
tasks~\cite{bavaresco2025,ahmed2025}, so a human-graded sample of verdicts is
needed and has not been done (Section~\ref{sec:future}). Nor do they
validate the key: both judges score against the same 520 defects. Whether
the key is complete and correct rests on the verification pipeline, the
independent-source checks (Section~\ref{sec:indep}) and the human audit not
yet done. The final comparison's reviews, which only the primary judge
graded, come from the same model family as that judge
(gpt-6.1-sol and gpt-6-sol); the second-judge check was not repeated on them.

\subsection{AACR's metric as we ran it}
\label{sec:aacrrun}

The harness's evaluation stage ran unmodified at a pinned commit
(\code{68a5697}), \code{all} variant, 3 rounds averaged, line gate $k=1$.
Its judge prompt was answered by \code{gpt-6-sol} at low reasoning through a
local OpenAI-compatible shim, not by the paper's
\code{Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507}~\cite{aacrbench}. The shim makes one
\code{pi -p} call per request and does not pass on the harness's sampling
parameters, so those are the model's defaults. Evaluation was sharded by PR
and merged with the harness's own summary functions; on a copy of a
finished run, sharded and unsharded results were identical per round,
average and PR. The AACR judge is noisy (the same pair was judged yes/yes/no
across three calls in testing), which is why three rounds are averaged.
\emph{Consequence:} our AACR table is comparable across our own rows, not
with the published leaderboard. We chose this for consistency: every
benchmark in this paper is graded by our own OpenAI judge rather than by the
judge each benchmark publishes, so that a score means the same thing on
AACR, Martian and Qodo. (The README describes how to point the shim at
Qwen3 through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint.)

\subsection{Choosing each third-party tool's configuration}
\label{sec:bestshot}

Our earlier study (Section~\ref{sec:sol}) ran each third-party tool as
shipped, or as the AACR harness runs it, on gpt-6-sol. A fairness audit of
those runs (2026-09-30) read each tool's documentation, shipped
configuration files and binaries, and every run's transcripts, and asked
whether each tool had been given its best shot. It found three that had
not: pi-review ran below its own default reasoning level and without its
panel, OpenCode ran through our compatibility bridge instead of its own
provider, and no tool but \qcr{} (and PR-Agent) was given the PR title and
body. The protocol (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}) measures each tool in the
configurations its own documentation ships or recommends, picks one per tool
by a rule fixed in advance, and measures what the pick itself is worth. It
follows the advice from machine learning that baselines get a tuning budget
comparable to the proposed method's, and that the choice itself is reported
with its optimism~\cite{melis2018,lucic2018,cawley2010}.

(13.9\%). \textbf{PR-Agent} finds 30--34\%, the fewest, with the highest AACR
precision (24--25\%), which is what a shipped cap of three key issues
produces. Against the published configurations the gap between \qcr{}
default and the best third-party tool on S40 was 20 points (86\% against
66\%); against the best configurations found it is 18--22 points (86--90\%
against 68\%). Giving every tool its best shot moved the field up; it did
not close the gap. These are 40 PRs and one run per configuration, and two
runs of one \qcr{} build differed by 4--8 points on single 10-PR sets
(Section~\ref{sec:stability}), so single-digit differences between rows are
not established.

\paragraph{Selection rule and the picks.} The rule, fixed before any
selection run: per tool, the configuration with the highest defect recall
on S40; a tie goes to the higher share of correct comments. The picks (bold
in Table~\ref{tab:bestshot}): pi-review's three-reviewer \code{code-experts}
panel at thinking high (68\%); open-code-review at effort high (68\%, tied on
defects found with medium plus PR text and ahead on comments correct, 76\%
against 73\%); OpenCode's native provider with the PR text (60\%); Codex CLI
at effort high (56\%); PR-Agent's shipped defaults on gpt-6.1-sol, the better
of its two models (34\%); and Claude Code unchanged (60\%). These are the
best configurations \emph{we found} among those the tools' documentation
ships or recommends, not an exhaustive search. Not run: Claude Code's
\code{/code-review} at a higher level and pi-review at \code{xhigh}.

\subsection{Deviations per tool, and their direction}
\label{sec:deviations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Codex CLI}: web search disabled could disfavour it, but no
  arm could search. Version 0.159.2 instead of the harness-era 0.158.0,
  because the ChatGPT backend refuses 0.158.0's requests for gpt-6.1-sol.
  Otherwise neutral.
  \item \textbf{OpenCode}: no harness runner exists; built in the Codex
  runner's shape on OpenCode's own provider and review command, with the
  Codex runner's findings fragment, so its reporting format is ours.
  gpt-6.1-sol is declared as a custom model on that provider, because
  1.18.33's catalogue predates it. It reads the PR title and body, which the
  harness never passes.
  \item \textbf{pi-review}: its panel at its shipped reasoning level, on pi
  0.99.1 with pi's new built-in extensions switched off; the diff is attached
  as a file because its reviewer has no shell. It does not read the PR text;
  on gpt-6-sol the PR text was worth 1.5 defects to it on S40
  (Table~\ref{tab:bestshot}), so this mildly \emph{disfavours} it.
  \item \textbf{Claude Code}: one configuration, a different model, and its
  read-only \code{git} commands were denied on 46 of 185 PRs by the
  harness's permission set, which may disfavour it. Its findings are parsed
  from prose (below); parsing errors mainly lower its AACR numbers and may
  move its comments-correct share either way.
  \item \textbf{open-code-review}: runs exactly as the harness's runner, at
  effort high set at our model bridge (1.3.1 has no effort flag).
  \item \textbf{PR-Agent}: run on a local git provider rather than GitHub,
  with its shipped defaults, including the 32k-token prompt budget.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Claude Code's prose parser.} In Claude Code 2.1.283,
\code{/code-review} is a bundled skill~\cite{claudecode}. It ignores the
harness's appended system prompt and its findings tool, and answers in
markdown prose, so the harness records zero structured findings (the logs
say \code{0 finding(s)} for 182 of the 185 logged cases). We wrote a
deterministic parser (\code{src/bench/claudeprose.ts}, 12 golden fixtures,
rules read off 150 real outputs) that reads \code{path:line} findings out
of the prose, applied after the fact without re-reviewing.
\emph{Rejected:} re-prompting Claude Code for structured output (that would
change the tool as shipped) and scoring it as zero (that would measure the
harness's integration, not the review). The defect judge reads the full
prose as the review body as well as the parsed comments, so defect recall
depends little on the parser; comments correct grades the parsed comments;
the AACR metric scores only parsed comments, so a finding without a line
number is lost there. The parser read 174 of the 185 reviews (the other 11
came back through the findings tool: 3 with findings, 8 empty) and
extracted 402 located findings (mean 2.3 per review, median 2, maximum 9; 29
reviews with none, mostly ``I found no bugs''). A hand check of 20 random
reviews found 45 findings, of which the parser extracted 44 with the right
path and line, and nothing that was not a finding; the miss names a file
but no line, which the rules leave out by design.\footnote{Computed from
\code{bench.claude\_prose\_parsed} in each result file and a reading of 20
sampled reviews (seed 20260929), 2026-09-29.}

\subsection{Execution and cost accounting}
\label{sec:cost-accounting}

Arms ran through a machine-wide scheduler with at most 10 concurrent
reviews per model login; the final third-party and \code{low} runs were
launched fresh in two halves (S40 and O145) and every case finished. Costs
are list-price equivalents, not bills. gpt-6.1-sol arms are priced at \$2
per million uncached input tokens, \$0.10 per million cached input tokens
and \$10 per million output tokens; gpt-6-sol arms the same with \$0.20 per
million cached tokens. For \qcr{} and pi-review these are pi's list prices,
summed over the main session and every pass; arms reporting OpenAI-style
usage (OCR, OpenCode and PR-Agent through the loopback bridge; Codex CLI
natively) have the cached count subtracted from input and priced at the
cached rate; Claude Code uses its own reported \code{total\_cost\_usd}, an
Anthropic list-price equivalent whose uncached column includes cache writes.
All OpenAI-model runs went through a ChatGPT subscription login, so what
they would have cost on OpenAI's API is unknown, and the relative cost of
OCR, OpenCode and \qcr{} low depends on the cached-token rate. Judging cost
is in Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\subsection{Tuned and unseen PRs; the reviewer-independent subset}
\label{sec:halves}
\label{sec:indep}

\qcr{}'s levers were designed on T40; R145 was never looked at for its
design. Every reviewer scores lower on T40 (the default 70\% against 81\% on
R145; the panel 62\% against 69\%; Claude Code 60\% against 63\%), so T40 is
harder for everyone (its PRs were selected by changed-line windows and, for 30 of them, a
minimum reference count, Appendix~\ref{app:sets}), and the default shows no visible
in-sample advantage. The default's recall minus the mean of the other seven
reviewers is 18.2 points on T40 and 22.5 on R145; in the earlier study the
same margin was 29.0 and 27.6 points (difference $+1.4$, 95\% CI $-6.3$ to
$+8.9$, Section~\ref{sec:sol}), so no tuning advantage is measurable, but
the intervals are wide enough to hide one of several points. Decisions taken
by looking at test data can overstate results on it~\cite{dwork2015}, while a
drop on fresh data alone does not prove overfitting~\cite{recht2019}; here
the fresh PRs are the easier ones, so neither effect is visible
(Figure~\ref{fig:halves}; per-set figures in
Table~\ref{tab:persetsfinal}).

The key was built partly from reviewers' comments (Section~\ref{sec:gt}).
On the 369 of the 520 defects (82 of the 90 high) that the expert hunt or an
AACR reference raised, which would be in the key had no reviewer's comment
ever been a candidate, the default finds 81\% and 76.5 of the 82
high-severity ones; then \code{low} 74\%, Claude Code 70\%, the panel 69\%
(75 of 82 high), Codex CLI 60\% (76 of 82 high), open-code-review 59\%,
OpenCode 58\% and PR-Agent 43\%. The default's lead over the next third-party
tool is 10 points. Every reviewer scores higher on this subset, which leans
towards defects easier to see from the diff. Two limits: membership in this
subset does not depend on any reviewer, but the \emph{definition} of a
defect, its severity and its ``counts if'' notes were still written by a
verifier with every reviewer's comments in view; and the stricter
leave-one-reviewer-out check was computed only for the earlier study
(Section~\ref{sec:sol}).

A less strict check keeps every defect that any source other than \qcr{}'s
own earlier runs raised: the hunt, an AACR reference, or the comment of
another reviewer (open-code-review's earlier runs on R145 and the four
extension reviewers). It drops the 28 defects (1 high, 3 medium, 24 low)
whose only sources are \qcr{}'s earlier default and \code{low} runs. On the
remaining 492 defects the order is unchanged and every reviewer's recall
rises, because the dropped defects are ones the others mostly missed: the
default 79.4\% (82.5 of 89 high), \code{low} 71.4\%, the panel 68.7\%,
Claude Code 65.7\%, open-code-review 59.0\%, Codex CLI 57.5\%, OpenCode
52.7\% and PR-Agent 39.1\%. The default's lead over the panel is $+10.7$
points [$+6.5$, $+14.7$] on this key, against $+11.8$ on the full key; the
default had 18.5 of its 409 credits on the dropped defects, the panel
9.5.\footnote{Computed for this check by
\code{paper-data/factcheck\_analyses.py} from the \code{provenance} fields
of \code{defects/*.json}, the per-PR candidate keys of R145
(\code{candidates-key.json}, \code{new-candidates-key.json}) and the
primary judge's verdict files; paired bootstrap as in Table~\ref{tab:ci}; no
model calls. On T40 no per-comment key survives, so every reviewer-comment
source there (\qcr{}'s and, on smoke10/dev10/holdout10, open-code-review's
earlier runs) is treated as \qcr{}'s; that drops 7 of the 28 and errs
against \qcr{}.} This is a leave-\qcr{}-out key, not a symmetric
leave-each-reviewer-out key: no other reviewer's own contribution is
removed.

\subsection{The frozen build}
\label{sec:finalbuild}

The comparison above is \qcr{} as of 2026-10-01 (build \code{b2ce03a}). It
was then changed for three more days and frozen (build \code{e32f0cf},
2026-10-04), on the same model, gpt-6.1-sol. The changes, described in the
companion report~\cite{qcrreport}: two lenses (one compares base and head
behaviour on every modified or deleted line of non-test code where base
behaviour is at risk; one is planned only when CI runs a linter or formatter
over a changed file); shared instructions to check a confirmed mechanism's
sibling sites and consequences, to anchor a finding at its cause, and that
no token, time or finding budget applies; a per-file coverage check that
sends a targeted sweep to changed source files no lens commented on; a
deterministic merge of duplicate findings into one comment per root cause;
the baseline, sweep and behaviour-comparison lenses at high reasoning
effort; and a prompt cleanup that cut the composed lens instructions by 32\%
in tokens.

Which sets its development used is listed at the head of
Section~\ref{sec:threats}. The build ran once on all 185 PRs and was judged
once by the primary judge; AACR-Bench's own metric was not computed for it.

\paragraph{A high-severity defect: a shared AST node re-parented

type-checking. The PR's conformance tests use only function hosts, which
is why they pass.

\subsection{Seeding bias: independent-source and leave-one-reviewer-out keys}
\label{sec:indep-sol}

The obvious objection to a pooled key is that seeding it with \qcr{}'s
comments inflated \qcr{}'s lead, since an in-pool reviewer gets credit for
everything it said that turned out to be true. This study, whose reviewers
were all in the pool, tests it in two ways.

\emph{The independent-source subset} is the 369 defects (82 high) that the
expert hunt or an AACR reference raised. 330 of them were also raised by a
reviewer, but their presence in the key does not depend on it.

them) every reviewer's comments were candidates, so ``no reviewer source''
means every reviewer missed them; on T40 (27) the provenance records only the
older runs from which those keys were built.

\emph{The leave-one-reviewer-out key} scores each reviewer only on the
defects that at least one source other than itself raised (the hunt, a
reference or another reviewer's comment), which removes each tool's own
seeding advantage symmetrically, as IR evaluation re-scores a system on a
pool built without its own contribution~\cite{zobel1998,buckley2007}. On
R145, where every comment is traced to its reviewer, the default's key
shrinks from 394 to 382 defects and its recall from 85.2\% to 84.7\%;
Claude Code's key drops to 386 and its recall from 63.3\% to 62.6\%; OCR (391
defects) moves from 59.5\% to 59.2\%; Codex CLI, OpenCode and pi-review (393
each) lose 0.1 points; \code{low} (394) does not move. On all 185 PRs the same
check moves Codex CLI, OpenCode, pi-review and Claude Code by 0.0--0.6
points. Own-seeding therefore explains at most 0.8 points of any reviewer's
recall in this study.\footnote{Computed from \code{provenance} in
\code{truth-ext.json}, the candidate keys per PR and the primary judge's
verdict files (\code{wp-data/prov.py}), 2026-09-29.} The check removes a
reviewer's \emph{own} contribution only; it leaves a sibling configuration's
(for \qcr{} default, \code{low}'s) and it was not repeated for the final
runs; for those, Section~\ref{sec:halves} removes both \qcr{}
configurations' contribution together.

\section{Why the two metrics disagree}
\label{sec:disagree}

Figure~\ref{fig:twoscores} shows the final comparison's reviews under both
metrics. On the defect benchmark \qcr{} default leads every other reviewer
by 8 points or more, and the field spreads from 37\% to 79\%. On AACR's
metric the default leads recall by under two points, open-code-review is
within one point of it on F1, and five reviewers that are 30 points apart
on defects (PR-Agent 37\% to the panel 67\%) are within 1.8 points of
each other on AACR recall (4.3--6.1\%); PR-Agent, last on defects, is
fourth on AACR recall. We see four mechanisms behind the difference, all
measured.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Most references are not defects.} About 19\% of labelled
  references name a real defect or a real maintainability issue; on R145
  only 12.8\% of references are part of a verified defect. A
  review that says only what is true and consequential cannot match the
  rest, and the rest are the majority of the recall denominator.
  \item \textbf{Most real defects are not references.} A true finding that
  is not on the list is a false positive under AACR precision; only 31\%
  of the verified defects have any reference among their sources. \qcr{}
  default posts the most comments (999) and the most true ones, so it pays
  the largest precision penalty. Not all of that penalty is the list's
  fault: by our own judge 56\% of the default's comments map onto a
  verified defect, the lowest share of the eight (\code{low} 58\%,
  open-code-review 63\%, the panel 67\%, PR-Agent 77\%, Claude Code 81\%,
  Codex CLI 85\%, OpenCode 87\%); the rest are mostly true minor notes
  (Table~\ref{tab:grades}).
  \item \textbf{The line gate.} A comment is judged only if it lands within
  $\pm1$ line of a reference. The right problem anchored two lines away is
  never judged. Tools that comment on line ranges (OCR, Codex, pi-review)
  get more candidate pairs than a tool that anchors single lines; \qcr{}'s
  posting shape scores a multi-line span at its single line.
  \item \textbf{Nits are rewarded.} AACR's references are largely minor
  notes. In the tuning log, iterations 1--4 raised AACR F1 by about 20\%
  while real defects found did not move: the extra matches were minor
  comments that happened to match the dataset.
\end{enumerate}

The list is also less human than it is labelled: at least 80 of its 362
``human'' references on our PRs are rewritten review-bot comments
(Section~\ref{sec:reflimits}). Removing them lowers AACR F1 by 0.1 to 1.4
points per reviewer and leaves the top three on recall and F1 as they were
(Claude Code passes PR-Agent lower down; Table~\ref{tab:aacr}), so this is not a fifth cause of the
disagreement; it bears on comparisons with the bots themselves.

In the pipeline check on three fresh10 PRs, each of the three reviewers
then run (default, low, OCR) scored 1 of 7 references (14\%) while the
defect table separated them by 3--4 real defects.

\paragraph{What AACR's metric is still good for.} It is the only number
computed the benchmark's way, and it shows category coverage: the
maintainability gain of the code-quality lenses is visible there (43.0
maintainability references matched by the default in the final
comparison, against at most 12.7 for a third-party tool), with the defect
table holding. A gain in AACR that
costs defect recall would indicate a reviewer drifting towards the list
and away from defects; we did not observe that.
\emph{The line-gate effect, measured} (on the earlier gpt-6-sol study's
reviews). We re-scored the reviews of all seven reviewers with the harness's matching at a line gate
of $\pm5$ and at $\pm999$, which in practice means any line of the same
file: no PR in the set changes more than 1{,}000 lines. This was one judge
round each, so it is compared with round 1 of the published $\pm1$
evaluation. References found, published $\pm1 \to \pm5 \to \pm999$:
default 167 $\to$ 171 $\to$ 190; low 134 $\to$ 146 $\to$ 165; OCR 148
$\to$ 151 $\to$ 171; Codex CLI 63 $\to$ 68 $\to$ 76; OpenCode 81
$\to$ 84 $\to$ 91; pi-review 83 $\to$ 86 $\to$ 97; Claude Code 80
$\to$ 85 $\to$ 113. Recall at $\pm999$ is 5.4--13.4\% (from 4.4--11.8\%)
and F1 8.4--16.2\% (from 7.0--14.1\%). Loosening the gate to any line adds 12--41\%
more matched references. The largest gain is Claude Code's (+41\%, whose
prose findings carry one line each), then low (+23\%) and Codex CLI
(+21\%); default gains +14\%. OCR leads F1 at every gate; at $\pm999$ low
overtakes default on F1 (14.7\% against 14.6\%) and Claude Code rises from
sixth to fourth on recall and from fifth to fourth on F1. Even with the line gate gone, no reviewer finds
more than 13.4\% of the references, so the gate explains only a small
part of the gap between AACR's metric and the defect table. The rest is
the reference list itself (Section~\ref{sec:reflimits}).\footnote{Computed
with \code{bench.sh eval -{}-line-k 5|999 -{}-rounds 1} (judge gpt-6-sol:low
through the shim, 3 in flight) on copies of each run's \code{results/all},
so the official $\pm1$ files were never touched. 3{,}227 and 10{,}983 judge
calls by the count taken at run time (2 failed and were scored as
non-matches; about \$18 at list price); the shim logs that survive record
3{,}368 of them, the rest having been overwritten. Scores from
\code{wp-data/linek\_scores.json}, 2026-09-30.}

\section{External check: Martian's offline benchmark}
\label{sec:martian}

AACR-Bench's references are one answer key; we ask whether what we found
there holds on a second benchmark built by different people with a different
key. Martian's offline code-review benchmark~\cite{martianbench} reviews 50
PRs from five projects (Sentry, Grafana, Keycloak, Discourse, cal.com) against
173 golden comments, about 3.5 per PR, each with a category and a severity.
Its leaderboard~\cite{martiansite} scores the published comments of the
commercial and open tools it lists, ranks the offline tab by $F_2$, and judges
with Claude Opus 4.5 by default. We did not change its key, its pipeline or
its scorer. The section ends with what we report from it and why
(Section~\ref{sec:martianpos}).

\subsection{How we ran it}

\paragraph{Pipeline.} Martian's pipeline fetches each tool's comments from
GitHub (step 1), has an LLM extract the distinct issues they raise, discarding
the location (step 2), merges duplicates (step 2.5), asks an LLM judge whether
each (golden, candidate) pair is the same issue (step 3), and scores
precision, recall and $F_\beta$ under three category profiles; the published
default is \emph{Core} (158 of the 173 goldens). We ran steps 2--3 and the
scorer from its repository at commit \code{e616e84}, unmodified, in a container
built from its own lock file. Step 1 is replaced by a converter that writes
\qcr{}'s posted review in exactly the record step 1 builds from GitHub,
including step 1's 30-item page limit; no review was posted anywhere. Each
case is the PR as the benchmark's forks carry it, the code every published
tool reviewed.

\paragraph{Reviewer and judge.} \qcr{} reviewed on gpt-6.1-sol, the model of
every other run in this paper, in its \code{default} configuration. The
judge is also gpt-6.1-sol: Martian publishes three judges (Opus 4.5,
Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.2) and none of them is the model our runs use, so the same
judge re-scores the \emph{published} comments of three published tools
(cubic-v2 and qodo-extended-v2, first and second on the Core leaderboard, and
Claude Code) on the same PRs, byte for byte from Martian's committed data; no
third-party tool was run. On ten PRs the re-judge of identical comments moves
by up to 4.3 F1 points from one pass to the next, sits 1.5--6.3 F1 points
below the published Opus 4.5 scores of the same comments, and keeps their
order. The other tools appear with Martian's published Opus 4.5
judgements, which we did not re-run.

\paragraph{Split.} Before any tuning we split the 50 PRs into
\code{tune20} (ten PRs picked by a size rule plus ten by a seeded hash) and
\code{test30}, the other thirty, held out. Every analysis below that reads
individual golden comments or candidates uses \code{tune20} only, except the
golden audit, which reads all 173 and is kept out of every reviewer change.

\subsection{What posting less does to the Martian score}
\label{sec:martianwhy}

\paragraph{Cutting volume does not close the gap.} Before the audit we tried
to raise \qcr{}'s Martian score by changing only what it posts. Over about 50
posting policies applied to the stored \code{tune20} reviews (no nits, each
finding once, a verdict-only review body, per-PR caps, severity floors,
agreement between lenses, validator confidence), $F_2$ stayed between 42 and
44 at best and fell with every cap, because the cap removed golden matches
along with the noise. The best policy by F1, frozen before the held-out PRs
were reviewed, went from F1 45.9 on \code{tune20} to 25.0 on \code{test30}
(Table~\ref{tab:martianraw}): its confidence threshold did not transfer. A
\code{snappy} profile that has the validator choose at most four comments per
PR reached 3.3 comments per PR and the same F1 as posting everything, with
$F_2$ down to 32.7. Even a perfect cut, raising precision to 60\% with no
loss of recall, would reach $F_2 \approx 59$ on \code{tune20}, below
cubic-v2's 69--70.

reviewer's severity bar drops such remarks by design.

\subsection{What the benchmark measures, and what it misses}
\label{sec:martiancritique}

Taken together, the results above point to eight properties of the
benchmark that a reader of its leaderboard should weigh.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{The key is half noise.} 77 of 173 golden comments are not
    real PR defects and 10 are disputed (Section~\ref{sec:martianaudit});
    tools earn 22--37\% of their credit on them (OpenCode on gpt-6.1-sol
    11\%), \qcr{} 25\%.
  \item \emph{A real bug off the list is a false positive.} On \code{tune20}
    eight tools found 44 real PR-introduced bugs that the list lacks; every
    one counts against the tool that found it.
  \item \emph{Minor and wrong are scored alike.} Precision cannot tell a
    noisy reviewer from a wrong one: 47\% of \qcr{}'s \code{tune20} comments
    are true but minor and 2\% wrong; cubic-v2's are 29\% and 14\%.
  \item \emph{Volume and phrasing move the score.} Extraction splits long
    comments, counts each repeated site and re-extracts a review body that
    restates its findings.
  \item \emph{Severity is labelled, inflated and unused.} 24 of the 66
    Critical or High goldens are not real, and missing a Critical golden
    costs the same as missing a Low one.
  \item \emph{The score depends on the judge.} Opus 4.5 credits 7--12 more
    matches per tool than gpt-6.1-sol on the same comments, and one judge
    moves by about three points between passes.
  \item \emph{The sample is small.} 50 PRs from five projects; a posting
    policy tuned on 20 of them fell from F1 45.9 to 25.0 on the other 30.
  \item \emph{The leaderboard rewards matching the list's style}: a handful
    of short, line-local remarks per PR, the trivial ones included. That is
    a reasonable product shape, and a different objective from finding the
    defects a PR introduces.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What we report}
\label{sec:martianpos}

We care whether a review finds the real defects a PR introduces, above all
the high- and medium-severity ones, and how many of its claims are wrong.
A true but minor remark costs a reader's attention; a wrong one costs more.
Martian's score measures agreement with a list of about three and a half
comments per PR, 45\% of which do not name a real defect, counts real defects
missing from the list and minor remarks the same as wrong ones, and ignores
severity. We therefore report three numbers side by side, none of them
alone: Martian's score as computed, unmodified and in full
(Table~\ref{tab:martianraw}); recall of the audited real goldens
(Table~\ref{tab:martianaudit}); and the extended-key results with strict and
lenient precision (Tables~\ref{tab:martianext} and~\ref{tab:martiansens}).
On all three the reading is the one AACR-Bench gave: \qcr{} finds the most
real defects, and its cost is volume, nearly all of it true but minor rather
than wrong. \qcr{}'s default is to find as many real problems as possible
and to post each with a severity; a team that wants fewer comments sets a
posting budget in its profile. That budget is a product choice about
reader attention, not a measure of review quality. Alongside the three
numbers we report the two severity views of
Sections~\ref{sec:martianstrong} and~\ref{sec:martianblocking}.

\section{External check: Qodo's PR-Review-Bench}
\label{sec:qodo}

Qodo's benchmark~\cite{qodobench} (Hugging Face \code{Qodo/PR-Review-Bench}
at \code{a73957c}, MIT) is 100 PRs from 8 repositories into which an LLM
injected 580 issues: 309 functional bugs and 271 violations of repository
rules, which Qodo extracted with an LLM and committed to every fork as an
\code{AGENTS.md} file. Every vendor's raw review is public on GitHub forks;
Qodo publishes no judge, prompt or scorer, so its published F1 cannot be
reproduced. We therefore judged every tool, \qcr{} included, with one judge
of ours under the matching rule Qodo's description states.

\paragraph{How we ran it.} Each case is the fork's PR rebuilt as two
synthetic commits, the merge-base tree and the head tree with neutral
messages, because the fork's branch names and history reveal the injection;
the \code{AGENTS.md} stays in the tree, as every vendor reviewed with it, and
the PR text is the upstream PR's own title and description. \qcr{} ran in
its frozen build (Section~\ref{sec:finalbuild}), \code{default}, on
gpt-6.1-sol, offline; nothing was posted. The nine vendors' inline review
comments were read with anonymous public API calls; they are the vendors'
January 2026 runs at default settings. The judge (gpt-6.1-sol at low
reasoning) receives, per PR and tool, the PR's issues and the tool's inline
comments and returns which comment states which issue, one to one; code then
checks location. The primary rule counts a match when the comment is on the
issue's file and its lines overlap the issue's within five lines; ``same
file'' and ``semantic only'' are sensitivities. Only inline comments count,
as in Qodo's rule; for \qcr{}, its inline comments and the review-body
findings that carry a path and line. Under ``semantic only'' our judge comes
within 0.1--1.3 F1 points of Qodo's published figure for six of the nine
vendors and keeps their order, the only calibration available.

\paragraph{Results.} The frozen build finds the most functional bugs of the
thirteen tools (52.1\%, against augment's 49.2\% and qodo-exhaustive's 48.2\%)
and the second-most issues overall (45.3\% against qodo-exhaustive's
46.4\%); it posts the most comments (7.1 per PR) at the lowest precision
(37.3\%), and its F1, 40.9, is fourth, behind Qodo's two configurations and
augment (Table~\ref{tab:qodo}). The change from the 2026-10-02 build is
almost all in rule violations, 14.8\% to 37.6\%, second to
qodo-exhaustive's 44.3\%: the frozen build checks every rule the repository
writes down and plans a lint-conformance lens when CI runs a linter, where
the earlier build treated most such items as nits it did not post. The
sensitivities do not change the order at the top: under ``semantic only''
the frozen build scores F1 46.7 (P 42.5, R 51.7, functional 61.2), against
qodo-exhaustive's 56.5 and qodo-precise's 51.2.

\paragraph{The same third-party harnesses.} The vendors' reviews ran on
models of their choosing. The three third-party harnesses of the
eight-reviewer comparison ran here in exactly that configuration, on
gpt-6.1-sol, which makes their rows a comparison of harnesses on one model
(Table~\ref{tab:qodo}, $\dagger$; the same three ran on Martian,
Section~\ref{sec:martian}). \qcr{} finds more than any of them: 45.3\% of
the issues against 27.2--29.0\%, 52.1\% of the functional bugs against
43.7--45.6\%, and 37.6\% of the rule violations against 8.1--12.2\%. They
post a third to a half as many comments at higher precision, and OpenCode's
F1, 40.0 (P 64.9), is within a point of \qcr{}'s 40.9; open-code-review's
is 35.2 and PR-Agent's 34.5. PR-Agent is Qodo's own open-source reviewer;
\code{qodo-exhaustive} and \code{qodo-precise} are its commercial product,
so this is PR-Agent's first row on the benchmark. Each costs \$0.10--0.46
and takes 1.2--2.3 minutes per PR, against \qcr{}'s \$2.48 and 19.6; these
PRs are larger than AACR's, so \qcr{}'s cost per PR is above its \$2.06
there.

\paragraph{Caveats.} Qodo built, injected and validated this benchmark, and
47\% of its issues are rule compliance, which its own product's rules
feature targets; the bugs are LLM-injected into real PRs, not organic. The
vendor reviews are January 2026 versions; \qcr{} is a build of October 2026.
The judge is ours, not Qodo's. A real defect that is not in the issue list
counts as a false positive, as on Martian: in a hand check of the 40
unmatched comments of the 2026-10-02 build on ten PRs (one model, the tool
hidden but recognisable by style), 12 were real defects the list lacks, 19
true but minor, 9 restated a listed issue or duplicated a credited comment,
and none was wrong. On the 31 PRs that none of the frozen build's
development used (Section~\ref{sec:threats}), its recall rose from 52.5\% to 60.3\% over
the 2026-10-02 build ($+7.8$ points, 95\% CI $+2.1$ to $+14.1$, paired
bootstrap over PRs) and its F1 from 49.7 to 53.0, against qodo-exhaustive's
51.3 on the same PRs.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

This section summarises \code{docs/THREATS-TO-VALIDITY.md}, ordered by
severity, and extends it to the final comparison. In short: the order at the
top of the final comparison (\qcr{}'s default first on overall recall) held
under every check we could run; the compared default's lead on high-severity
defects is not established over the panel or Codex CLI (the frozen build's
is); several threats push the measured lead in \qcr{}'s favour, while one
(the pool, Threat~2) pushes against every final run. The authors build \qcr{}; vendor-run evaluations
are known to favour their sponsor even when standard risk-of-bias checks
pass~\cite{lundh2017,deepsourcebench}, and interested parties bias even
third-party leaderboards~\cite{singh2025leaderboard}, so none of the
mitigations below substitutes for an independent replication.

\paragraph{Development sets (disclosure).} \qcr{}'s September levers were
designed from its misses on T40, including holdout10 and fresh10, each also
used for later decisions (Appendix~\ref{app:chron}); R145 was selected
mechanically after the last of those changes and never opened for them, and
the two halves are reported apart (Section~\ref{sec:halves}). The frozen
build's changes were designed from the compared runs' misses on R145, on
Martian's \code{tune20} and on 59 Qodo issues a vendor found and the
2026-10-02 build missed, and validated on smoke10 and dev10; holdout10,
fresh10, Martian's \code{test30} and 31 of Qodo's PRs were not used. On
those 31 Qodo PRs its recall rose 7.8 points over the 2026-10-02 build
[$+2.1$, $+14.1$] (Section~\ref{sec:qodo}).

\paragraph{Threat 1: the key was built partly from our reviewer's output (severe; measured).}
Recall is against the union of defects somebody in the candidate pool raised
and a verifier confirmed. \qcr{} default (on gpt-6-sol) raised 88\% of the v1
R145 defects and 29 that nobody else did. \emph{Mitigations:} anonymised,
shuffled candidates with the key withheld; every rejection recorded (2{,}118
and 691); few additions from the extension. \emph{Measured:} on the 369
defects the hunt or a reference raised, the final comparison's order at the
top is unchanged (default 81\%, \code{low} 74\%, Claude Code 70\%, the panel
69\%; Section~\ref{sec:halves}); in the earlier study the
leave-one-reviewer-out key moved no reviewer's recall by more than 0.8 points
(Section~\ref{sec:indep-sol}); on a key without the 28 defects only
\qcr{}'s earlier runs raised, the final order is unchanged and the default
leads the panel by 10.7 points (Section~\ref{sec:halves}). \emph{Open:}
the other final reviewers were not re-scored on leave-own-out keys; a
blind-first key built before any
reviewer runs is the only way to remove pool bias by construction, the same
bias pooled relevance judgements carry in IR~\cite{zobel1998,buckley2007}.

\paragraph{Threat 2: the final runs are outside the pool (moderate; indicated, not measured).}
No final gpt-6.1-sol run's comments were candidates for the key, so a real
defect that only those runs found is credited to nobody
(Section~\ref{sec:outofpool}). The judge flags 219 such possible defects,
0.02 to 0.33 per PR per reviewer, against at most 2 per reviewer on R145 for
the in-pool runs. Every final reviewer's recall is understated by its share;
the default has the most flags in absolute terms, three third-party tools
the most per comment. The flags are unverified. \emph{Open:} extending the
key with the final runs' comments.

\paragraph{Threat 3: the competitors' configurations (moderate; measured in part).}
Each third-party configuration was picked as the best of two to four single
runs on S40, then carried from gpt-6-sol to gpt-6.1-sol without
re-selection. \emph{Measured:} on O145 the picks move by $-11$ to $+4$ points
and the order is unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:selection}); the transfer held
within run-to-run noise for four of five picks and failed for OpenCode, whose
pick lost 10 points (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). \emph{Not measured, and
plausibly against the tool:} Claude Code was not given the protocol at all.
It stayed at its medium-effort \code{/code-review}, which its documentation
describes as reporting only its most confident findings~\cite{claudecode};
its read-only \code{git} commands were denied on 46 of 185 PRs; and it runs
on a different model. Its 62\% is therefore not a best-shot figure.
\emph{Open:} the candidate lists are what each tool's documentation ships or
recommends, not an exhaustive search; OpenCode's other variants were not
re-run on gpt-6.1-sol. Each configuration is our reading of that tool's
documentation, not its maintainers' recommendation; corrections from
maintainers will be published as errata.

\paragraph{Threat 4: no human-verified key and no human-validated judge (severe; unmeasured).}
Claude agents did every step of the key; critics changed 6 things in the
first pass and 4 in the extension. No human has checked any part of the
defect key, any judge verdict, or the AACR reference labels on the 30
original PRs, which agents produced (Section~\ref{sec:reflimits}). If
the verifier accepted plausible-but-wrong defects, recall is wrong for every
reviewer, and the reviewers whose comments seeded those defects benefit;
severity grades, which drive weighted recall and the high-severity results,
are also unchecked. Two LLM judges agreeing is not agreement with
humans~\cite{bavaresco2025,shankar2024}. \emph{Mitigation:} every defect
carries quoted-code evidence and every exclusion a reason, both committed, so
an audit is possible; a blinded audit of 20 defects and 5 exclusions is
prepared (Section~\ref{sec:gt}), and is smaller than the stratified sample
that would bound the error rate where the lead is concentrated.

\paragraph{Threat 5: one designer for the reviewer, the key's definition and the judge (moderate; partly measured).}
The same assistant designed \qcr{}'s levers, wrote the admission rule for
defects (including when maintainability counts), wrote the ``counts if''
notes and the judge prompt, and orchestrated the key. Independent-source
subsets test whether \qcr{}'s comments \emph{seeded} the key, not whether
the key's \emph{definition} of a defect matches \qcr{}'s design. The lead is
concentrated where such an alignment would show: low severity and
maintainability (Section~\ref{sec:results}). \emph{Measured in part:}
without the 72 maintainability defects the default still leads the panel
(78\% against 71\%); on high and medium defects only, its lead over the panel
is 2.3 points, 95\% interval [$-2.7$, $+7.0$] (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the
frozen build's lead extends to both (Section~\ref{sec:finalbuild}). \emph{Open:} an admission rule and judge prompt written
independently of \qcr{}'s design.

\paragraph{Threat 6: model-family overlap between judge, key and contestants (moderate).}
The primary judge (gpt-6-sol) shares a family with the seven OpenAI-based
reviewers, including ours; the key builder and the second judge share a
family with Claude Code. LLM judges can prefer their own family's
outputs~\cite{panickssery2024,zheng2023}. In the earlier study a second
judge from another family agreed on 98.7\% of verdicts and moved no
reviewer's recall (Section~\ref{sec:judgestab}); the one visible same-family
effect was on comments correct. That check was not repeated on the final
comparison's reviews. The key half is not addressed by judge agreement at
all.

\paragraph{Threat 7: unequal inputs, effort and budget (moderate; direction known).}
In the final comparison \qcr{}, OpenCode and PR-Agent read the PR title and
body; pi-review, open-code-review, Codex CLI and Claude Code do not (their
harness invocations have no channel for it, or their selection runs did
better without it). \qcr{} default spends 2.6 to 21 times the cost of each
third-party tool and writes 5.1 to 24 times its output tokens; Claude Code
runs a different and more expensive model. Most of these favour \qcr{} on
defects found; the cost column states the budget difference, and the
companion report~\cite{qcrreport} varies \qcr{}'s own budget. A strict
equal-budget comparison is not done.

\paragraph{Threat 8: per-tool harness deviations (moderate, per tool).}
Documented in Section~\ref{sec:deviations}. In the earlier study the
pi-review and OpenCode deviations disfavoured those tools by 7 (OpenCode's
bridge) and 10 points (pi-review's reasoning level)
of recall on S40; the best-shot protocol removed them. The Claude Code parser
mainly affects AACR numbers.

\paragraph{Threat 9: one run per reviewer (moderate; measured for \qcr{}).}
Every reviewer in the final comparison ran once, and the bootstrap intervals
cover PR sampling. For \qcr{} we measured the run-to-run spread: a full
reproduction on 185 PRs came within 1.5 defects, while six runs on the same
8 PRs found between 11 and 14 of 17 defects and two runs of an older build
differed by 4--8 points on single 10-PR sets (Section~\ref{sec:stability}).

\paragraph{Threat 10: metric construction (moderate for AACR, low for ours).}
AACR: reference quality, the line gate, a non-paper judge, and ``human''
references that are at least 22\% rewritten review-bot comments (removing
them moves F1 by at most 1.4 points and does not change the leaders;
Table~\ref{tab:aacr}). Ours: partial credit as $\tfrac12$ (counting it as
zero moves no reviewer by a point); weighted recall's 3/2/1 weights are a
convention; the same judge grades recall and comment accuracy; the judge sees
exclusions written from the tools' own comments.

\paragraph{Threat 11: order of decisions (low).} The model of the final
comparison, gpt-6.1-sol, was chosen after \qcr{}'s 185-PR result on it was
known. \qcr{} finds 5 points \emph{fewer} defects on it than on gpt-6-sol, so
the choice does not favour it, but it was not fixed in advance. The selection
rule and set were fixed before any selection run.

\paragraph{Threat 12: data handling (low).}
Eleven unrunnable PRs excluded for every arm; OCR under-credited on fresh10
in the key's construction; different benchmark-harness builds across arms (a
later \qcr{} build reproduced the original default run within 1.5 defects);
runs stopped and resumed in chunks, and one stalled by an auto-suspend; one
accidental local run of the real \code{claude} binary during parser work, in
a container with no network and no credential, so no model call was made.

\paragraph{Threat 13: training-time contamination (unknown; cannot be measured here).}
AACR-Bench's PRs, their follow-up fixes and their review threads are public,
so any reviewer's model may have seen them in
training~\cite{sainz2023,riddell2024}. The sandbox prevents only the
review-time leak. Every reviewer is exposed to the same risk, but not
necessarily equally. A key built on PRs merged after every model's cutoff, as
time-segmented code benchmarks do~\cite{jain2025livecodebench,zhang2025swebenchlive},
would remove the threat.

\paragraph{Things we could not verify.} The real billing price of
gpt-6-sol, gpt-6.1-sol and gpt-6-luna behind the subscription login (every
OpenAI-model cost is a list-price computation); wall-clock time per PR
without contention; whether Claude Code's \code{/code-review} behaves the
same on a subscription token as on an API key (we used an API key); whether a
newer harness commit would run Codex or Claude Code differently.

\subsection{What we claim and what we do not}

\paragraph{We claim} that on AACR-Bench's 185 runnable PRs, under our
defect judge and our verified key, \qcr{}'s frozen build finds 97\% of the
high-severity defects, 94.5\% of the high- and medium-severity ones and 89\%
of all 520, more than every other reviewer we ran at every severity, at the
highest cost and time of the eight (\$2.06 and a median 17.5 minutes per
PR); and that on Martian's audited real goldens and on Qodo's functional
bugs it has the highest recall of the tools scored by one judge, including
the same three third-party harnesses on the same model, with the lowest
Martian F1 and the fourth-highest Qodo F1, at \$2.84 and \$2.48 per PR
against their \$0.09--0.86. We claim that in the
eight-reviewer comparison, with every third-party tool except Claude Code in
the best configuration we found among those its documentation ships or
recommends and every OpenAI-based reviewer on gpt-6.1-sol, \qcr{}'s default
finds more of the key's defects than every other reviewer we ran (79\%
against 37--70\%), with every paired difference on overall recall excluding
zero over PR resampling; that it ranks first on the PRs not used to
choose the configurations (O145), on the PRs not used to tune it (R145), and
on the 369 defects the key would hold without any reviewer's comment; that
its lead is concentrated in low-severity defects; and that it has the highest
share of correct comments, a third of them minor. In the earlier study, with
every tool as shipped on gpt-6-sol, it also ranked first, under a second
judge from another model family as well, and a re-run on a later build
reproduced its result. We claim that on AACR's own metric the reviewers are
close, because that metric mostly rewards reproducing a list that is largely
not real defects, and that every other reviewer is cheaper per defect found.

\paragraph{We do not claim} a lead of the compared default on high-severity
defects over pi-review's panel or Codex CLI; absolute recall; an exact size
of the lead; that the final runs' recall is free of the out-of-pool undercount; that the best
configurations we found are the best that exist for each tool, or that
Claude Code ran at its best; that the lead comes from the multi-pass design
rather than from budget or the PR description; a human-verified key or a
human-validated judge; a like-for-like comparison at equal compute; or
comparability with the published AACR leaderboard.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A code-review benchmark is only as good as its answer key. The answer key
AACR-Bench ships, the comments its PRs received, mostly written or rewritten
by models, is a poor list of those PRs' defects: most of its entries are not
defects, most real defects are not on it, and at least a fifth of its
``human'' entries are rewritten review-bot comments. Replacing it with a
verified, severity-graded defect key and a judge that reads whole reviews
changes what the benchmark can see: eight reviewers that AACR's metric
squeezes into a few points spread from 37\% to 79\% of the key's defects, the
spread is stable across two judge families (on an earlier set of reviews)
and across the PRs used and not used for any decision, and the best-shot
protocol showed how much an evaluator can move a competitor by configuration
alone (12 to 17 points for three tools on S40).

The authors' own reviewer, \qcr{}, is designed for recall, and recall is
where it leads. Its frozen build finds 97\% of the key's high-severity
defects and 89\% of all of them, ahead of every reviewer compared at every
severity; on Martian's audited real goldens and on Qodo's functional bugs it
has the highest recall of the tools scored by one judge, ahead of the same
three third-party harnesses on the same model on both. The price is stated
beside it: \$2.06 and a median 17.5 minutes per PR, 51\% more and 1.8 times
as long as the build of the eight-reviewer comparison and several times
every third-party tool, and more comments, many of them true but minor,
which gives it the lowest Martian F1 and the fourth-highest Qodo F1. The weakest link is the key: built by agents of
one model family, partly seeded by that reviewer's earlier output, defined by
the same assistant that designed it, not reaching the final runs' own finds,
and not yet checked by a human. Until a human audit is published, the key's
recall figures are best read as agreement with a careful, documented,
machine-built answer key, not as ground truth.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:future}

\paragraph{The key.} A blinded human audit, stratified by severity and
provenance; extending the key with the final runs' comments,
which would turn the out-of-pool flags into credited or rejected defects; and,
to remove pool bias by construction, a key built from a blind hunt alone on
PRs from outside AACR-Bench, merged after every model's training cutoff,
before any tool runs. The AACR reference labels on the 30 original PRs need a
human check too.

\paragraph{The judges.} A human-graded sample of judge verdicts, and a re-judge with an independently written prompt and
independently written ``counts if'' notes, which would test the bias the two
current judges could share. On AACR's side, re-scoring with the paper's own
judge, with a published leaderboard baseline re-run under both judges, would
place our AACR numbers against the public leaderboard.

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.} No per-component ablation of \qcr{} on
R145; no \qcr{} run without the PR text; no strict equal-budget arm; no
Claude Code at a higher review level or with read-only \code{git} commands
allowed; OpenCode's other variants not re-run on gpt-6.1-sol.

\paragraph{Cost and time.} Every OpenAI-model cost is a list-price
computation for runs made through a ChatGPT subscription; the Anthropic arms
are API list prices. Wall-clock minutes were measured under a shared
scheduler, never per PR in isolation.

\section{Prompts (summary)}
\label{app:prompts}
\sloppy

The full text of every prompt that produced a number is in
\code{bench/aacr/docs/PROMPTS.md}, rendered from the saved workflow
scripts, not retyped. Summary:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ground truth, first 30 PRs}: one blind hunter per PR, one
  consolidator merging the hunt, labelled references and anonymised
  reviewer comments, one critic per set; v2 adjudicator for the judge's
  ``new defect'' candidates plus a critic.
  \item \textbf{fresh10}: four hunt agents, one finalise agent checking
  hunt drafts (H), anonymised reviewer comments (C) and references (A),
  instructed to be even-handed between hunt and reviewer candidates.
  \item \textbf{rest145}: the stopped blind hunt; the final hunt with
  references as leads (never reading run outputs); the independent
  verifier (never opening the candidate key); the critic; a single-PR
  re-verification; the four-reviewer extension verifier and critic (``you
  only ADD'').
  \item \textbf{Defect judge}: the prompt in Section~\ref{sec:judging},
  identical on both judge models.
  \item \textbf{AACR judge}: the harness's own yes/no ``same concern''
  prompt, unmodified, answered through the local shim.
  \item \textbf{\qcr{}}: the production session system prompt and
  round-first order, the addendum registry (42 addenda; the ones written
  during this work are \code{quality-cap-validate},
  \code{validate-demote-not-kill}, \code{every-problem-on-the-lines},
  \code{validate-restate-not-kill}, and two \code{nits-open} addenda that
  are off in every scored run), the quality lenses, and the
  \textsc{benchmark mode} override: no GitHub, CI gate not applicable,
  decide on the merits, write \code{comments.json}, \code{review.md},
  \code{findings-record.json} and \code{outcome.json} instead of posting;
  the PR title and body are included as untrusted data.
  \item \textbf{Other reviewers}: OCR's command line (its prompts are in a
  compiled binary); the harness's Codex and Claude runners; OpenCode's
  built-in review with the Codex findings fragment; pi-review's own
  review contract.
  \item \textbf{Parsers}: deterministic, no model: per-arm conversion to
  \{path, line range, note\}; the Claude Code prose rules.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{The defect-judge prompt}
\label{app:judgeprompt}

The prompt below is the one every scored judgement used (judge label
\code{v2-gpt-6-sol-high}; \code{judgePrompt} in \code{src/bench/defects.ts},
unchanged since ground truth v2). It is reproduced verbatim except that each
interpolated value is shown as an \code{<UPPERCASE>} placeholder. Each
defect renders as \code{<ID> [<severity>, <kind>] <title>}, then its
statement, its sites (\code{path:from-to (role)}) and, when present, its
``counts if'' note; each comment as \code{<ID> [<tag>] path:line}, then its
text. The rejected-claims block appears only when the PR has rejected
claims; the body line only when the review has a body.

\begin{lstlisting}
You are the judge of a code-review benchmark that measures one thing: does a review FIND the real defects in a pull request and point them out, and is what it says true? Wording, tone, tags and exact line numbers do not matter; substance does.

THE PULL REQUEST: <TITLE> — <REPO>, head <HEAD_SHA>, merge base <MERGE_BASE_SHA>. The repository is checked out at the PR head in your working directory. You may read any code (read tool; bash for git diff/show/log/grep, rg, ls, find, sed -n, head, tail, wc). Never run project code.

THE REAL DEFECTS (ground truth — each verified against the code; trust these):
<DEFECTS, or: (none — this PR has no real defect in the ground truth)>

CLAIMS ALREADY CHECKED AGAINST THE CODE AND REJECTED (not defects — each with the reason it failed). A comment repeating one of these is never a new defect: judge it incorrect, partly-correct or correct-minor as its reason says, reading the code if the comment adds something the reason does not cover:
<REJECTED CLAIMS, one per line: - <SOURCE>: <REASON>>

THE REVIEW (what the author received):
Verdict: <VERDICT, or (none)>
Body:
<BODY>
Comments:
<COMMENTS, or (no comments)>

YOUR TASK
1. For EACH defect decide whether the review found it:
   - found: some comment (or the body) identifies THIS defect — the same underlying problem, with its essential point right (see "counts if"), so the author would fix it. It may sit at the cause or the symptom site, or name the site in its text.
   - partial: the review points at this problem or its site, but with a wrong or missing mechanism or consequence, so the author could fix the wrong thing.
   - missed: nothing in the review identifies it.
2. For EACH comment: which defect it finds (its id, or null), and whether it is correct. Check every comment that finds no listed defect against the code:
   - correct-valuable: true at the head and worth raising before merge;
   - correct-minor: true but low value (style, a test request, a nice-to-have, a nit);
   - partly-correct: a real problem, but the mechanism, scope or severity is materially wrong;
   - incorrect: false at the head (the code does not do what it says, or the scenario cannot happen);
   - unverifiable: cannot be decided from the repository.
   A comment that finds a listed defect is correct-valuable, or partly-correct if it found it only partially. Set new_defect true only for a correct-valuable comment that describes a real defect that is neither in the list nor among the rejected claims.
Be strict both ways: do not credit a comment for sounding plausible, and do not dismiss one without reading the code it cites.

Answer with ONE JSON object and nothing after it:
{"defects":[{"id":"D1","verdict":"found|partial|missed","comments":["C2"],"why":"one sentence"}],"comments":[{"id":"C1","defect":"D1","verdict":"correct-valuable|correct-minor|partly-correct|incorrect|unverifiable","new_defect":false,"why":"one sentence with file:line"}]}
Every defect id and every comment id above must appear exactly once.
\end{lstlisting}
