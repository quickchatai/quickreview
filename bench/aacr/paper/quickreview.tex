\begin{abstract}
QuickReview is an automated pull-request reviewer designed for recall: to
find as many of a change's real problems as possible, the serious ones
first. It rests on a bet that a review assembled from many narrow,
independent reads of a change, each checked against the code before it is
posted, finds more of them than one long read does. For each PR one session
plans about ten lens passes from the change by explicit rules --- a
baseline, a tests lens, a line-by-line sweep over every changed file, four
code-quality lenses and narrow sub-lenses triggered by what the diff or the
PR text says --- runs each as an isolated read-only agent, merges what they
raise, and has a validator re-derive every surviving candidate from the
code before one review is composed. We measure it on a verified defect
benchmark over the 185 runnable PRs of AACR-Bench, built by the same authors
in a companion paper. Its frozen build finds 97\% of the 90 high-severity
defects (87.5, a partial find counting half), 94.5\% of the high- and
medium-severity ones and 89\% of all 520, ahead of every other reviewer at
every severity; the strongest third-party configuration, pi-review's
three-reviewer panel, finds 89\%, 85\% and 67\%. On two external benchmarks
it has the highest recall under one judge: 87\% of the golden comments of
Martian's offline benchmark that an audit judged real, and 52\% of the
functional bugs of Qodo's PR-Review-Bench (45\% of all its issues, second
to 46\%), ahead on both of the same three third-party harnesses run on the
same model (they find 54--78\% of the real goldens and 44--46\% of the
functional bugs). Recall is bought with comments,
money and time. It posts 6.9 comments per PR, 92\% of them correct but 42\%
minor, which gives it the lowest Martian F1 and the fourth-highest Qodo F1;
it costs \$2.06 per PR at list prices (median \$1.87; \$2.84 and \$2.48 on
Martian's and Qodo's larger PRs), 51\% more than the build of the main
comparison and 4 to 29 times the third-party tools; and
it takes a median 17.5 minutes per PR, 1.8 times that build, against 0.7--2.3
minutes for the others. The design is studied on that earlier build, which
finds 79\% (95\% interval 75--82\%) at \$1.36 per PR, ahead of the panel's
67\% almost entirely on low-severity defects. Most of its spend is in
redundant lenses, whose value is very uneven: the per-file sweep alone found
29 defects no other lens raised, while two code-quality lenses cost a tenth
of the budget for one such defect. With the design fixed, the base model
moves recall from 28\% to 84\%; no equal-budget alternative was run.
Because the authors built the benchmark partly from this reviewer's own
earlier output, we state where that could inflate these numbers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A code reviewer, human or automated, is asked to find the problems a change
introduces before it is merged. Automated reviewers built on large language
models now ship from tool vendors, open-source projects and model
providers~\cite{codex,claudecode,ocr,pragent,pireview,opencode}, and most of
them share one shape: an agent reads the diff, explores the repository as
it sees fit, and writes its findings in one pass. That shape has a
characteristic way of failing. When we analysed the defects our own
reviewer kept missing during development (48 real defects across 30 PRs
that several runs failed to find;
Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}), most were never raised by any part of the
review at all, not raised and then discarded. Fifteen were attention
failures: the code was in front of the model and it read past a misspelled
name or an unchecked identifier. Eleven needed knowledge of what a
third-party library actually does, which a reviewer that never installs
anything cannot look up. Several needed a procedure applied exhaustively ---
every consumer of a widened check, every pairing of old and new versions
--- that a general read does not apply.

QuickReview\footnote{Its code, configuration and earlier reports call it
\code{quickreviewd}.} is our answer to those failures. It runs every day on
the authors' production repository: a daemon receives pull-request events and,
for every PR a team asks it to review, one session writes one review with
inline comments, a verdict, and a section of points for a human to
double-check. Its design rests on three choices. A review is the union of
many narrow, independent passes rather than one long read, so that a defect
one pass misses can be raised by another. Every candidate is re-derived from
the code by a separate validator before anything is posted, so that the
volume of candidates does not become volume of noise. And the reviewer only
reads code, CI results and library sources; it never executes anything.
Those choices cost compute, and the question this report answers is what
they buy for it.

\paragraph{Recall first.} Because the design aims at finding as many real
problems as possible, we lead with recall --- of high-severity defects
first, then of all defects --- and report precision, comment accuracy, cost
and time beside it as the price of that aim. Table~\ref{tab:frozen} puts the
shipping build's recall, cost and time next to the strongest competitors'.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How many verified defects does \qcr{} find, how
  accurate is what it says, and how does it compare with the review tools a
  team could adopt instead, each at its best configuration and on the same
  model? (Section~\ref{sec:qresults})
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] What does it cost, where does the cost go, and what
  does each part of the design contribute? (Section~\ref{sec:cost})
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How does it compare with the third-party design
  closest to it, pi-review's three-reviewer panel, and what can that
  comparison say about design versus budget? (Section~\ref{sec:panel})
  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] How much of the result is the model under the design?
  (Section~\ref{sec:basemodel})
  \item[\textbf{RQ5}] How stable is the result across runs?
  (Section~\ref{sec:stability})
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) A precise description of a multi-pass review
design: its lens catalogue and the rules that plan each lens, its validator
and the dispositions it can give a candidate, and its model tiers and
presets (Section~\ref{sec:design}). (2) Its evaluation against seven other
reviewer configurations on a verified defect benchmark, with intervals,
severity, kind and size breakdowns and the benchmark's own metric. (3) A
decomposition of its cost into lenses, validator and session, with per-lens
attribution showing which redundancy pays and which does not. (4) A
comparison with the closest competitor design on all 185 PRs, separating
what the data show from what they only suggest. (5) A base-model study and
two stability checks.

\paragraph{Conflict of interest.} The authors built \qcr{}, and the same
team, using agents directed by the same assistant that designed the
reviewer's tuning levers, built the benchmark's answer key --- partly from
candidate defects in \qcr{}'s own earlier reviews --- and wrote the judge's
prompt. The companion paper~\cite{qcrbench} measures how far this could
inflate the results; Section~\ref{sec:limits} repeats what was measured and
what was not. We report every number we have, including those that do not
favour \qcr{}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Human code review.} Studies of modern code review find that it
is valued for knowledge transfer and design discussion as much as for
defects, and that most of what reviewers raise concerns maintainability
rather than functional bugs~\cite{bacchelli2013,mantyla2009,sadowski2018}.
\qcr{}'s code-quality lenses follow that finding: they look for what will
cost the next reader, not only for what breaks. Its lens structure has an
older ancestor in software inspection: perspective-based reading assigns
each inspector one stakeholder's perspective and a procedure to follow, and
reported higher team defect detection than with the reading technique it
replaced~\cite{basili1996}. A \qcr{} lens is a
perspective with a procedure, executed by a model.

\paragraph{LLM code review in practice and research.} Industrial reports
describe LLM reviewers deployed at scale: Google's AutoCommenter flags
violations of coding best practices in review~\cite{autocommenter}, and a
study of an LLM review tool in industry found that developers acted on many
of its comments while noting noise and longer review times~\cite{cihan2025}.
Research systems range from models pre-trained for review
tasks~\cite{codereviewer} to multi-agent designs in which agents with
different roles discuss a change~\cite{codeagent}. The tools compared here
are agents that read the diff and the repository, with one exception that
is itself multi-angle: pi-review's panel runs three specialists and keeps a
finding two of them agree on~\cite{pireview}. Benchmarks for these systems,
and why we built a verified defect benchmark rather than reuse reference
comments, are discussed in the companion paper~\cite{qcrbench}.

\paragraph{Redundancy, ensembles and verification.} Sampling a model several
times and aggregating the answers improves accuracy on reasoning
tasks~\cite{selfconsistency}, more sampled agents with majority voting keep
helping as their number grows~\cite{moreagents}, and coverage --- the chance
that at least one of many samples is right --- grows steadily with the
number of samples~\cite{monkeys}. Coverage needs a way to pick what is
right: trained verifiers~\cite{cobbe2021}, a model answering its own
verification questions~\cite{cove}, or several models debating to a common
answer~\cite{debate}. Self-correction without external feedback is
unreliable~\cite{huang2024}; \qcr{}'s validator is meant to get its feedback
from the code, re-deriving each claim by reading it. Two differences from
this literature matter. \qcr{}'s passes are not samples of one prompt but
different prompts, so they aim at different defects as well as at the same
one; and their errors are not independent --- the classic result for
independently written program versions is that faults coincide far more
often than independence predicts~\cite{knight1986}, which bounds what
redundancy can buy.

\paragraph{Cost-aware evaluation.} Agent benchmarks that report accuracy
alone reward spending; cost belongs beside accuracy, and comparisons should
be read on the cost--accuracy frontier~\cite{kapoor2024}. Routing work to
cheaper models where they suffice can cut cost at equal
quality~\cite{frugalgpt}. We report cost per PR, per defect found and per
additional defect, and we compare against alternatives that are cheaper,
not only better.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

This section describes the reviewer at the build every final number was
measured on (\code{b2ce03a}); later commits change the reviewer's code, and
a reader reproducing the results should use that build.

\subsection{Principles}

\begin{description}[style=unboxed,leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=3pt]
\item[Independent angles.] A review is the union of narrow passes, each a
leaf agent that starts from the PR's diff and worktree, holds read-only
tools, and does not see the other passes. Passes never launch other agents;
the PR's main session is the only scheduler.
\item[A check before posting.] A separate validator re-derives every
candidate from the code. Lenses are told that a plausible but wrong finding
costs more than a missed nit, and the validator's job is to remove what does
not survive contact with the code.
\item[Read, never run.] Every pass carries an execution policy: it never runs
project code, tests, builds or package managers. Anything that needs
execution is CI's job, and in production the reviewer reads CI results. To
read what a library does, the round is given the library's source (below),
not an environment that runs it.
\item[Narrow procedures over broad instructions.] Where a class of defect
needs an exhaustive procedure --- every consumer of a widened check, every
file through a round trip --- a dedicated sub-lens exists whose whole charter
is to fill that table, so that a skipped row is visible rather than silent.
\end{description}

\qcr{} runs one main session per PR (Figure~\ref{fig:round}). The session
receives a \emph{round order}: the PR's merge base and head, its title and
description, a \emph{pass plan} computed by deterministic code from the
changed files and the PR text, a list of \emph{addable} lenses, and the
location of any unpacked dependency sources. It then follows the round
template's steps.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sync and dispatch.} The session launches every planned pass
  as a leaf agent, each with a task string naming its focus. It may
  \emph{raise} a pass to a higher reasoning tier when the change's semantic
  complexity warrants it, and \emph{add} an addable lens when it can name a
  concrete reason; it may never drop a planned pass or lower a tier. Raises
  and additions are recorded with their reasons.
  \item \textbf{Merge.} The session collapses candidates that describe the
  same defect into one (\emph{merge}, keeping every raising lens), and
  carries a \emph{different} defect on the same changed lines as its own
  paragraph inside the surviving comment (\emph{fold}). A P1 or P2
  candidate is never folded.
  \item \textbf{Validate.} The validator receives every candidate and the
  merge decisions, and gives each a disposition (Section~\ref{sec:validator}).
  \item \textbf{Decide and compose.} The session maps severities to posting
  tags, decides the verdict and writes the review: an opening paragraph, a
  section of points for a human to double-check, the verdict line, and at
  most 50 inline comments, each anchored inside a changed-line run of the
  diff (a finding with no valid anchor goes into the body).
  \item \textbf{Record.} It writes a findings record of every candidate,
  the lens that raised it, its evidence and its disposition.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Severity and posting.} Every finding carries a severity on a
fixed scale (P0: certain production breakage, data loss or security hole;
P1: a bug or contract violation that must not merge; P2: a probable defect
needing an answer; P3: worth raising, not blocking; nit), a confidence, a
claim and its evidence. The posting map is P0/P1 $\to$ \code{[blocker]};
P2 $\to$ \code{[blocker]} at confidence $\ge 0.8$, otherwise
\code{[question]}; P3 $\to$ \code{[question]} or \code{[nit]}. A
reachability axis adjusts this: a finding whose trigger is contrived never
blocks, and a P2 whose trigger is merely plausible posts as a question. Any
surviving blocker makes the verdict REQUEST\_CHANGES; otherwise the verdict
is APPROVE. In \code{strict} nit mode (the default) the validator drops
true-but-trivial candidates and a nit must name the change it asks for.

\paragraph{Behaviour lives in addenda, not prompt rewrites.} Each lens is an
agent file (a shared contract plus the lens's own instructions). Changes to
a lens's behaviour are composed onto the built agent as named addenda from
one registry (\code{PASS\_ADDENDA}), scoped to all passes, to the lenses, or
to named passes. At the measured build the registry holds about forty
addenda, among them the execution policy, read batching, the reachability
rules, the severity caps of the judgment and quality lenses, the validator's
closed vocabulary of kill reasons and its demote, restate and doubt rules
below, and a rule that a lens which finds one problem on some lines must
look again for a second one.

\paragraph{Dependency sources.} For the third-party packages a PR imports,
the round downloads the packages' source archives at the versions the PR's
lockfiles pin, from the npm and PyPI registries only, and unpacks them as
plain text files; nothing is installed and no tool from an archive runs.
The round order lists where they landed, so a pass can read and cite the
library rather than its memory of it. A failure of any kind becomes a
skipped entry; the round never waits on it.

\subsection{The validator and its dispositions}
\label{sec:validator}

step's decisions. For each candidate it re-derives the claim by reading the
code, checks it against points already settled in the PR's discussion,
verifies the anchor, audits severity and confidence, and merges residual
duplicates. Each candidate ends in one disposition
(Table~\ref{tab:dispositions}).

fold & a different defect on the same changed lines, carried inside the surviving comment & inside it \\
doubt & the claim fails: \code{refuted} (counter-evidence read), \code{unverifiable} by reading, \code{bad-anchor}, or confidence below 0.5 & no (kept in the record) \\
kill & a repeat of a settled point, out of scope, below the bar (a change that would not improve the code), or the wrong shape (a nit that asks for nothing, self-narration, style without a written guide) & no \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\emph{Doubt} replaced deletion for validity vetoes between the gpt-6-sol
runs and the measured build: a doubted candidate stays in the findings
record, marked as possibly invalid, so that a validator at a lower tier
cannot make a stronger lens's finding vanish without trace. For what reaches
the review a doubt and a kill are the same, and the mechanism figures in
Section~\ref{sec:cost}, measured on the earlier build, count validity vetoes
as kills.

\subsection{Models, tiers and presets}
\label{sec:presets}

Every pass runs at one of three tiers. The two cheaper tiers are chosen by
diff size (below 150 changed lines, or above), the third is high; in the
\code{default} preset both cheaper tiers map to medium reasoning, so the
tier label records diff size rather than a reasoning level. The sweep and
intent start at the cheapest tier, the three risk passes and the validator
run at high, and the main session runs at the model's default (medium).
The validator's tier is fixed and is never raised to match a lens.

One configuration word, the \emph{preset}, sets the reasoning level and the
lever plan (Table~\ref{tab:presets}); an explicitly set key overrides it. The
\code{default} and \code{low} presets share the \emph{lean} plan, which was
tuned for equal recall at lower cost (Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}); \code{max}
enlarges it.

\subsection{What the benchmark measures, and what it leaves out}
\label{sec:benchmode}

The benchmark runs the production round, not a benchmark build: the same
round template rendered by the production renderer, the same session
persona, the same plan computation, the same built lens and validator agents
with every addendum, and the same model configuration (with the base model
replaced through configuration overrides). It differs in what cannot run
offline, and those differences are stated to the session in a
\textsc{benchmark mode} section appended to the round order:

\begin{itemize}
  \item There is no GitHub, no CI and no memory. The CI gate on approvals is
  off; the session decides on the merits. Reviewer memory is off so that a
  foreign repository never reads or writes the production reviewer's memory.
  \item The session posts nothing. It writes four files instead: the inline
  comments exactly as it would post them, the review body, the findings
  record, and an outcome file with the verdict. The defect judge reads the
  comments and the body.
  \item The repository profile is the generic one, not the profile of the
  authors' production repository, whose framework-specific rules would be
  noise on these projects.
  \item The PR's title and description are given, as in production;
  dependency sources are fetched; one container runs each PR with a
  45-minute limit.
  \item Production features outside a single first review are absent:
  rechecks after approval, replies to authors, escalation, the migration
  pre-check.
\end{itemize}

\noindent A switch that posts every true nit (\code{[review] nits = "open"})
exists because AACR-Bench's own metric rewards nits; it is off in every
scored run.

\section{Evaluation setup}

\subsection{Where the lead comes from}

\paragraph{By kind of defect.} The largest margin is on maintainability
defects (72 defects): the default finds 81\% against 16--51\% for the
third-party tools, which fits the code-quality lenses. Those lenses were
designed on the tuning PRs, so this is also where an alignment between the
design and the key's notion of a defect would show first. Without the
maintainability defects the default finds 78\% against 71\% for the panel.
On incomplete fixes (57 defects) it finds 89\% against 73\%. On the largest
class, functional bugs (229 defects), the margin is small: 77\% against
73\% for the panel and 68--69\% for Claude Code and open-code-review.
\footnote{Scratchpad \code{paper-data/final-tables.md}, \S3b and \S4.}

\paragraph{By PR size.} The lead grows with the size of the change. On PRs
of at most 50 changed lines the default finds 70\%, against 67\% for Claude
Code and 62\% for the panel; on 51--200 lines 76\% against 69\%; on
201--1{,}000 lines 82\% against 67\% for the panel and 60\% for Claude
Code. A reviewer that reads every changed file in small groups should gain
most where a single read has most to skim past; the per-file sweep is the
candidate mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).

\paragraph{Tuned and unseen PRs.} \qcr{}'s levers were designed from its
misses on the original 40 PRs; the other 145 were never looked at before its
final build. It scores lower on the 40 (70\%) than on the 145 (81\%), and so
does every other reviewer, so the 40 are harder for everyone. Its margin over
the mean of the other seven reviewers is 18.2 points on the 40 and 22.5 on
the 145, the opposite of what tuning on the 40 would produce. On the 145
PRs not used to choose the competitors' configurations it leads the panel
77\% to 66\%.

\paragraph{Without reviewer-sourced defects.} On the 369 defects the key
would hold if no reviewer's comment had been a candidate (those raised by
the code hunt or a dataset reference), the default finds 81\%, \code{low}
74\%, Claude Code 70\% and the panel 69\%. On a key that drops only the 28
defects (24 low) whose sole sources are \qcr{}'s own earlier runs, the
default finds 79.4\% of 492, \code{low} 71.4\% and the panel 68.7\%; the
lead over the panel is $+10.7$ points [$+6.5$, $+14.7$] and no order
changes~\cite{qcrbench}.

\subsection{AACR-Bench's own metric}
\label{sec:aacr}

On the benchmark's published metric --- how closely a review reproduces the
dataset's 1{,}418 reference comments on these PRs, at least 80\% of them AI-written --- the default has the highest recall (10.7\%) and F1 (12.5\%);
open-code-review is next on F1 (11.6\%), and PR-Agent (21.3\%) and
open-code-review (20.2\%) have the highest precision against the default's
15.2\%. With the 80 references that are rewrites of earlier review bots'
comments removed, the default and open-code-review stay first and second
on F1 (11.4\% and 10.8\%). The companion
paper~\cite{qcrbench} explains why every number on that metric is low and
why its order differs from the defect benchmark's.

\section{Cost: where it goes and what it buys (RQ2)}
\label{sec:cost}

\qcr{}'s default spends \$1.36 per PR at list prices: 2.6 times
open-code-review, 5 times the panel, 7--9 times Claude Code, OpenCode and
Codex CLI, 21 times PR-Agent; it reads 1.88M input tokens per PR (75\% of
them cached) and writes 27k, 5.1--24 times the output of the third-party
tools (Figure~\ref{fig:costcov}). Unless stated, the mechanism figures in
this section are from the gpt-6-sol default run (Table~\ref{tab:sources}),
on which the default cost \$1.71 per PR and found 437.5 defects; the
gpt-6.1-sol run's split is unmeasured.

\subsection{Where the money goes}

The lenses take 75.5\% of the cost, the validator 10.9\% and the main
session 13.5\%. Within the lenses the spend follows how often a lens runs
and how much it reads (Table~\ref{tab:lenscost}): the sweep alone is 13.6\%
of the total, the baseline 9.4\%, the tests lens 7.1\%, and the four
code-quality lenses together 22.1\%.

\paragraph{Redundancy is large and deliberate.} Of the 440 defects credited
to the default on the gpt-6-sol run,\footnote{The attribution counts a
partly found defect once; Table~\ref{tab:bm185} counts it as half.} only 71
were raised by a single lens and 127 by six or more (two lenses 57, three
72, four 60, five 53). The validator saw 2{,}959 candidates: 1{,}456 merged
as duplicates of another finding --- the redundancy, collapsed --- 1{,}034
posted, 164 demoted and posted, 207 killed (7.0\%), 64 withheld and 34
folded.

\paragraph{Its value is uneven.} A lens's \emph{unique} finds --- defects
for which every crediting comment names that lens alone --- are the best
offline proxy for what dropping it would lose (Table~\ref{tab:lenscost}).
Two lenses carry most of them: the sweep (29 unique finds, 2 of them high,
for 13.6\% of the cost) and maintainability (15, mostly stale documentation,
for 5.4\%). At the other end, abstraction and duplication together cost
9.9\% of the budget for one unique find in 185 PRs; placement, bounds,
contracts and style found nothing no other lens found. Shared credit is
large for every lens --- consistency co-found 237 defects --- which is the
redundancy above: a lens with no unique finds may still make a find more
likely in a run where the lens that usually raises it misses.
\footnote{Per-lens offline analysis of the gpt-6-sol default run's findings
records, 2026-09-29 (scratchpad \code{tuning-writeup.md}, Q1--Q3). ``Unique''
is fragile: the other lenses' overlap varies from run to run.} Dropping the
sweep is estimated to cost about 5.6 points of recall.

elsewhere in the file, 7 had nothing raised in the file, and 6 had a
candidate at the site that was dropped. Posting every kill would add 23\%
more comments for at most 1.5 points.

\subsection{What the compute buys}

\paragraph{With the design fixed, less compute finds less.} The \code{low}
preset --- the same plan, every role at low reasoning --- finds 70\% at
\$0.88 per PR against 79\% at \$1.36; the difference is established. On the
gpt-6-sol runs, where the gap was larger (84\% against 66\%), an attribution
of the lost credit put about 40\% of it in lens generation (fewer
candidates near the defect), about 20\% in validator filtering (a low
validator killed 26 defects' only candidates against 5 at high), and about
40\% in weaker comments that were near the defect but earned no credit. A
smaller base model that writes about half as much per pass collapses to
28\% (Section~\ref{sec:basemodel}): what the lenses do not raise, the
validator and the session cannot recover.

\paragraph{Marginal cost.} Average cost per defect favours cheap reviewers
by construction; the figure that matters for choosing a configuration is the
cost of the defects one buys by moving up. Over the 185 PRs, moving from
\code{low} to the default costs about \$89 more for 43.5 more defects (about
\$2.05 each, 4 of them high severity); moving from the panel to the default
costs about \$201 more for 61.5 more defects (about \$3.27 each), 56 of them
low severity.\footnote{Computed from the whole-benchmark list costs (\$251,
\$162, \$50; scratchpad \code{paper-data/final-tables.md}, \S5) and
Table~\ref{tab:final}.}

\paragraph{The counterpoint.} \qcr{}'s case is recall, not cost efficiency.
Every other tool is cheaper per defect found (\$0.06--\$0.33, against \$0.61
for the default and \$0.44 for \code{low}; Table~\ref{tab:ci}). On
high-severity defects alone the panel and Codex CLI are not separated from
the default, and they find a high-severity defect for \$0.62 and \$0.33
against \$3.01. A team that wants the most important findings cheaply is
better served by one of those tools at its best configuration; \qcr{} is the
choice when a missed medium- or low-severity defect costs more than about
\$2--3 of review. All costs are list prices; the OpenAI-based arms ran
through a subscription login, so they are not bills.

\section{The closest competitor: pi-review's panel (RQ3)}
\label{sec:panel}

Of every third-party configuration we ran, pi-review's three-reviewer panel
comes closest to \qcr{}, and its design is also the closest. This section
compares the two designs, the results on all 185 PRs, and what the
comparison can and cannot say about design versus budget.

\subsection{Two designs}

The panel is \code{pi-review -{}-mode code -{}-panel code-experts}, which is
what pi-review's \code{/rv} command runs by default in the pi
host~\cite{pireview,pi}. The \code{code-experts} preset defines three
reviewers, for correctness, security and testing; each is an isolated,
read-only pi process with its own role prompt, and all three review the same
diff without seeing each other. Their findings are clustered --- by a
deterministic match on path and summary, then, for ambiguous pairs on the
same path, by a model adjudicator that may merge findings but not invent,
drop or rewrite them --- and a cluster raised by at least two reviewers is
\emph{confirmed}; a singleton stays visible as an \emph{advisory}.
Table~\ref{tab:designs} sets the two designs side by side.

\subsection{Results on all 185 PRs}

The panel finds 347.5 of 520 defects (67\%) against the default's 409
(79\%), 80 of the 90 high-severity defects against 83.5, at a fifth of the
cost. Of the 61.5 defects between them, 56 are low severity, 2 medium and
3.5 high. The default's lead is established ($+11.8$ [$+7.8$, $+15.7$]);
\code{low}'s 3.5 points over the panel, at 3.2 times its cost, are not.

\paragraph{What the vote does.} We score confirmed and advisory findings
alike, because both are in the panel's output; this is the generous reading.
Re-reading the same judgements with confirmed clusters only, the panel finds
250 of 520 defects (48\%) and 69 of 90 high-severity ones; the singletons
carry 97.5 of its 347.5 credited defects. The vote does not separate right
comments from wrong ones: 90\% of confirmed comments are correct (264 of 292)
against 88\% of advisories (259 of 293). The confirmed-only figures re-read
the judgements rather than re-judge; a panel that posted only confirmed
findings could be graded slightly differently.\footnote{FULL-BENCHMARK.md,
``Why pi-review's panel is the strongest competitor''.} So the panel's
recall comes from its independent angles, including the angles that only
one reviewer took, and not from its agreement check.

\paragraph{The model behind both.} On the 40-PR selection set, where we also
read the panel's clustering output, both systems ran on gpt-6-sol and
gpt-6.1-sol (Table~\ref{tab:panel40}). The panel's comment accuracy rose
from 78\% to 91\% with the newer model; its recall moved by two defects.
One pi-review reviewer at high had 71\% of its comments correct and 62\%
recall on gpt-6-sol, the panel 78\% and 68\% --- at 2.7 times the cost.

\subsection{Why \qcr{} finds more: candidate explanations}

Four differences could account for the low-severity gap. None was ablated
on the unseen PRs, so these are hypotheses with partial support, not
findings.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The per-file sweep.} The panel has no pass that walks every
  changed file; each specialist decides where to look. On the gpt-6-sol run
  the sweep was the only route to 29 credited defects, 22 of them low, and
  dropping it is estimated to cost about 5.6 points. The lead growing with PR
  size is consistent with this.
  \item \textbf{More passes and more reading.} About ten lenses to three
  reviewers, 5.3 times the input and 5.7 times the output.
  \item \textbf{A validator that demotes.} A real find by one \qcr{} lens is
  posted, demoted if in doubt; a real find by one pi-review specialist stays
  an advisory. Under our scoring both count, so this explains a difference
  in what reaches a developer's attention more than a difference in recall.
  \item \textbf{More context.} \qcr{} reads the PR text and dependency
  sources. For pi-review the PR text was a small lever: one reviewer at high
  gained 1.5 defects from it on the 40 PRs, and the variant lost its
  selection.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The comparison is asymmetric in both directions: \qcr{} had more
context and five times the budget; the panel's reviewers ran at high
reasoning where \qcr{}'s lenses ran at medium.

\subsection{Design or budget?}
\label{sec:designbudget}

The strongest competitor is the design closest to \qcr{}'s, several
independent angles followed by a decision over what they raised. It is
tempting to read that as evidence that the approach, and not only the
budget, carries the result. The data support a narrower statement.

\emph{What the data show.} Independent angles help pi-review: three
reviewers find more than one, and the extra recall comes from findings only
one reviewer raised. Within \qcr{}, budget and model matter a great deal:
\code{low} loses 8.4 points, a small model 56 (against gpt-6-sol). Spending more does not by
itself buy recall in every tool --- OpenCode through our bridge cost \$0.96
per PR on gpt-6-sol and found 43\% on the 40, open-code-review at high cost
\$1.12 and found 68\%, and the panel matched the latter at \$0.46 --- but
those are differences between tools, not between budgets for one design.

\emph{What they do not show.} Every comparison that adds angles also adds
budget: the panel costs 2.7 times one reviewer, \qcr{} five times the panel.
We ran no equal-budget alternative --- a panel with as many reviewers as
\qcr{} has lenses, a single reviewer at maximum effort, Claude Code at its
broader effort levels --- and no lens ablation on the unseen PRs. Whether
\qcr{}'s design beats a different way of spending \$1.36 per PR is open.
\NEEDEXP{an equal-budget arm, e.g.\ a 9--10-reviewer pi-review panel or a
single high-effort reviewer at about \$1.36/PR, on the 145 unseen PRs}

the 40 are \qcr{}'s tuning PRs.

\section{The frozen build}
\label{sec:frozen}

Everything above measures build \code{b2ce03a} (2026-10-01). Over the next
three days the reviewer was changed once more, from the misses of that build
on the stored benchmark runs, and frozen as build \code{e32f0cf}
(2026-10-04), still on gpt-6.1-sol. The changes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Two lenses.} A behaviour-delta lens compares base and head on
  every modified or deleted line of non-test code, planned only where base
  behaviour is at risk (a removed or replaced guard, condition, call,
  return or default, a reordered statement, existing code wrapped in a new
  branch, a deleted file). A lint-conformance lens is planned only when a CI
  job runs a linter or formatter over a changed file.
  \item \emph{Shared instructions.} Once a mechanism is confirmed, check its
  sibling sites, its twin, every consequence and the other failures in the
  same loop; anchor a finding at its cause, not its symptom; report one-token
  slips on changed lines (an operator, a bound, a loop target, a
  near-namesake); and no token, time or finding budget applies. The style
  lens walks every rule the repository writes down.
  \item \emph{Coverage.} After the lenses, changed non-test source files on
  which no lens raised anything get a targeted sweep; a wide change's sweep
  is packed by a line budget rather than capped by file count.
  \item \emph{One comment per root cause.} A deterministic merge clusters
  findings that share a site, a named identifier or a fix target, and only
  across different validator groups, so it never overrules the validator.
  \item \emph{Effort.} The baseline, the sweep and the behaviour-delta lens
  run at high reasoning effort.
  \item \emph{Prompt cleanup.} Contradicting rules resolved in favour of the
  newer, shared addenda merged and compressed: the composed lens instructions
  are 32\% shorter in tokens. No lens was removed.
\end{itemize}

minutes for the third-party tools. It also says more: 6.9 comments per PR,
42\% of them true but minor, and 4.5\% graded incorrect against 2.5\%.

\paragraph{External checks.} On Martian's 50 PRs, scored by one
gpt-6.1-sol judge with three tools' published comments, the frozen build
finds 75 of the 86 golden comments an audit judged real (87\%), against 68
for qodo-extended-v2 (level with it on the \code{test30} half, 43 of 52
each), and has the highest Martian recall (65.8) and the lowest precision
(16.2). On Qodo's PR-Review-Bench, with all thirteen tools judged by our judge, it
has the highest recall of functional bugs (52.1\%) and the fourth-highest F1
(40.9); its recall of rule violations rose from 14.8\% to 37.6\%, and on the
31 Qodo PRs its development did not use, its recall rose by 7.8 points over
the 2026-10-02 build (95\% CI $+2.1$ to $+14.1$). Both benchmarks count a
real defect missing from their lists as a false positive~\cite{qcrbench}.

The same three third-party harnesses as in the main comparison
(open-code-review, PR-Agent and OpenCode, each in its configuration there
on gpt-6.1-sol) also ran on both benchmarks, which makes those rows a
comparison of harnesses on one model. On Martian's audited real goldens
they find 67, 46 and 48 of 86 against the frozen build's 75; on Qodo's
they find 27--29\% of all issues against 45.3\%, 44--46\% of the
functional bugs against 52.1\%, and 8--12\% of the rule violations against
37.6\%. They win on precision and F1: on Martian all three have a higher F1
than the frozen build's 26.1 (33.1--38.8), and open-code-review a higher
$F_2$ (47.4 against 40.9) and full coverage of the real Critical and High
goldens on one more PR (16 of 19 against 15); on Qodo OpenCode's F1, 40.0,
is within a point of the frozen build's 40.9. They cost \$0.09--0.86 and
take 1.2--3.3 minutes per PR on these benchmarks, against the frozen
build's \$2.84 and 26.1 minutes on Martian and \$2.48 and 19.6 on Qodo.

\section{What these numbers do not show}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The benchmark's authors built this reviewer.} The answer key was
built partly from candidate defects in \qcr{}'s own earlier reviews (on gpt-6-sol it raised 88\% of the defects in the first build of the
key for the 145 new PRs, 29 of them raised by no other source), by agents orchestrated by the
same assistant that designed its levers, who also wrote the judge prompt.
Measured: on the defects the key would hold without any reviewer's comment
its lead holds, and in the earlier study scoring each reviewer only on
defects some other source raised moved no reviewer by more than 0.8
points~\cite{qcrbench}. Not done: a human audit of the key, and a key built
before any reviewer ran.

\paragraph{No human has verified the key for the 145 new PRs.} Claude
agents built and critiqued it; severity grades, which drive the
high-severity results, are likewise unchecked by a person.

\paragraph{The final runs are outside the key's pool.} None of the final
gpt-6.1-sol runs' comments were candidates for the key, so a real defect
only they found is credited to nobody. The judge flags 61 possible such
defects in \qcr{} default's reviews and 4--49 in each other out-of-pool
reviewer's (47 for the panel, 49 for open-code-review); per 100 comments
three third-party tools have more than \qcr{}'s 6.1. These flags are
unverified, and they could move the gaps in either direction by a few
points~\cite{qcrbench}.

\paragraph{One judge, sharing a model family.} The final reviews were graded
by gpt-6-sol only; the second judge, from another family, agreed on 98.7\%
of verdicts in the earlier study but did not grade these reviews. The judge
shares a family with every reviewer here except Claude Code.

\paragraph{Development sets.} Every September lever was designed from
\qcr{}'s misses on the original 40 PRs; the other 145 are reported apart and
show no in-sample advantage, though no lens was ablated on them. The frozen
build's changes were designed from misses on the 145, on Martian's
\code{tune20} and on Qodo's PRs, and validated on 20 of the original 40; the
other 20, Martian's \code{test30} and 31 of Qodo's PRs were not used
(Section~\ref{sec:frozen}).

\paragraph{Budget and inputs.} \qcr{} spends 2.6 to 21 times the cost of
each third-party tool and reads the PR title and body, which four of the
other tools did not in the final runs. We have not separated how much of the
lead comes from the design and how much from the budget and the description
(Section~\ref{sec:designbudget}).

\paragraph{One run per reviewer.} Every reviewer ran once. For \qcr{} a full
re-run reproduced the gpt-6-sol result and repeat runs bound its spread on 8
PRs. The competitors' configurations were chosen on gpt-6-sol and
applied unchanged on gpt-6.1-sol.

\paragraph{Not the production configuration.} The measured reviewer ran
with the generic repository profile, no memory, no CI gate, and none of the
production behaviour after a first review; the reviewer's code has changed
since build \code{b2ce03a}, and the frozen build of Section~\ref{sec:frozen}
is the one that ships.

\paragraph{Nothing about people.} The judge grades whether a comment is
true and whether a defect was found. We did not measure whether developers
act on the comments, how long the extra minor comments take to read, or what
a false alarm costs a team.

\paragraph{Scope.} Public open-source PRs of at most about 1{,}000 changed
lines in ten languages, reviewed once each. Very large changes, private
codebases and multi-round review are outside what was measured.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\qcr{} is built for recall: it reviews a change through about ten narrow,
independent lenses chosen by explicit rules, and posts only what a validator
can re-derive from the code. On a benchmark that counts verified defects,
with every competitor at its best documented configuration and the
OpenAI-based ones on the same model, its shipping build finds 97\% of the
high-severity defects and 89\% of all of them, ahead of every competitor at
every severity, and it has the highest recall on the real goldens of
Martian's benchmark and on the functional bugs of Qodo's, ahead of the same
three third-party harnesses on the same model on both. That recall has a
price, stated with it: \$2.06 and a median 17.5 minutes per PR, several
times every competitor, and more comments, many of them true but minor,
which costs it precision on both external benchmarks. The build on which
the design was studied found 79\% at \$1.36 per PR, with the largest share
of correct comments (95\%); its extra spend over the closest competitor
bought mainly low-severity coverage, and on high-severity defects
pi-review's panel and Codex CLI were level with it at a fifth and a ninth of
its cost per high-severity defect found.

The cost decomposition is the part of this report we would most want a
designer of such systems to take away. Redundancy is what makes the design
work --- most defects are raised by several lenses, and the validator mostly
collapses duplicates rather than rejecting claims --- but its value is very
uneven: a line-by-line sweep over every changed file earns its share many
times over, while two of the code-quality lenses cost a tenth of the budget
for almost nothing no other lens found. The misses that remain are
recognition misses at the lenses, not filtering.

The closest competitor is also the closest design, several independent
reviewers and a decision over what they raised. That is consistent with the
view that independent angles plus a check carry the result, but every
comparison that adds angles here also adds budget, and the model under the
design moves recall from 28\% to 84\%. Whether this design beats a
different way of spending the same budget is the question this report
leaves open.

The frozen build (Section~\ref{sec:frozen}) is what closed the
high-severity gap: lenses at the sites where base behaviour changes,
instructions to exhaust a confirmed mechanism, a coverage check and higher
effort on three passes took recall from 79\% to 89\%, at 51\% more cost and
1.8 times the time.

\section{Tuning history in brief}
\label{app:tuning}

The chronological experiment log (a working note) records the measurements that
shaped the shipped default. We summarise the points relevant to judging
overfitting; all are single runs on small sets on gpt-6-sol and are
superseded by the full benchmark.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Prompt iterations 1--5 did not measurably change defect-finding
  (every version 49--54.5 of 97 defects on 30 PRs); they raised AACR F1.
  Two runs of one build differed by 2.5 defects.
  \item Reasoning effort was the largest single lever measured before the
  levers existed: every model at maximum effort found 24.5 of 34 defects on
  smoke10 (one run) against 15.5--17.5 for eight normal-effort runs, at about
  three times the cost and time. It was not shipped as default.
  \item The missed-defect analysis behind the levers traced 48 defects the
  review kept missing on the 30 PRs: 42 were never raised by any pass, 15 were
  attention failures, 11 needed library knowledge. The levers (sub-lenses,
  sweep, dependency sources) raised found defects from an expected 30.5
  (mean of seven earlier normal-effort runs) to 45.5 of 67 on the 16
  in-sample PRs (one run), and from 20.5 to 25.0 of 29 on fresh10 (one run
  per arm) --- about half the in-sample gain per PR.
  \item The lean plan kept recall at lower cost and became the
  \code{default} preset; \code{low} costs about half and gives back most of
  the levers' gain.
  \item The tests lens and the ``every problem on the lines'' rule raised
  fresh10 recall to 27 / 26.5 of 29 over two runs --- by then fresh10 was
  in-sample.
  \item The code-quality lenses added about 3 problems found on 9 PRs
  (mostly low severity, within noise) and raised AACR maintainability matches from 1 to 4 of 30, at +29\% cost; abstraction posted nothing on its
  own there, the first sign of what Table~\ref{tab:lenscost} later showed.
\end{itemize}
